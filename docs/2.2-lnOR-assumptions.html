Effect Sizes for Dichotomous Data: Assumptions and Interpretation

Odds Ratio, Risk Ratio, Risk Difference, and Hazard Ratio

Published

October 28, 2026

Sometimes the world is just black and white. Individuals live or die. Organisms are present or absent. Vegetation plots burn or don’t burn.

Calculating effect sizes for dichotomous data starts with organizing the counts into a 2×2 table. In ecology, this usually compares an exposed (or managed) group with a control group, across an outcome with two states (e.g., mortality vs. survival, or presence vs. absence):

Target Event (e.g., Mortality) Non-Event (e.g., Survival) Total
Exposed (e.g., Pollutant / Stressor) a b a + b
Control (e.g., Ambient Conditions) c d c + d

We’ll use this running example throughout:

# 2x2 tables for amphibian chytrid infection in two wetlands
dat_dichotomous <- data.frame(
  site = c("Wetland A", "Wetland B"),
  ai = c(15, 20), # Infected, exposed (warmed mesocosm)
  bi = c(85, 30), # Uninfected, exposed (warmed mesocosm)
  ci = c(10, 15), # Infected, control (ambient)
  di = c(90, 35)  # Uninfected, control (ambient)
)

knitr::kable(dat_dichotomous)
site ai bi ci di
Wetland A 15 85 10 90
Wetland B 20 30 15 35

We’ll cover the three main effect sizes for these data: the log odds ratio (lnOR), the log risk ratio (lnRR), and the risk difference (RD).

Important

If the event can happen multiple times to the same individual (e.g., the number of times a plot burns or a bird breeds), use incidence rate ratios (see Multiple events per individual). If you’re interested in when the event happened, not just whether it did (e.g., days until nest predation), use hazard ratios (see Time-to-event data).

Log odds ratio (lnOR)

The odds ratio (OR) compares the odds of the event in the exposed group with the odds in the control group. Odds are the probability of the event happening divided by the probability of it not happening, and range from 0 to infinity. An OR of 1 means the odds are the same in both groups. Using the cells of the 2×2 table:

\[ OR = \frac{a \times d}{b \times c} \tag{1}\]

ORs are not analyzed directly in a meta-analysis because they are bounded at 0 and are not normally distributed. For example, an OR of 0.5 and an OR of 2 are equal and opposite effects, but they are not the same distance from 1. Log transforming fixes this: lnOR is centered on 0 and ranges from \(-\infty\) to \(\infty\).

\[ lnOR = \ln(OR) \tag{2}\]

Calculating lnOR

dat_or <- escalc(measure = "OR", 
                 ai = ai, bi = bi, 
                 ci = ci, di = di, 
                 data = dat_dichotomous)
dat_or

       site ai bi ci di     yi     vi 
1 Wetland A 15 85 10 90 0.4626 0.1895 
2 Wetland B 20 30 15 35 0.4418 0.1786

Interpreting lnOR

To interpret lnOR (or a pooled estimate and its confidence interval), exponentiate it (\(e^{lnOR}\)) to convert it back to an OR. For Wetland A:

or_a <- exp(dat_or$yi[1])
or_a
[1] 1.588235
  • OR = 1: Equal odds of the event in both groups (e.g., the stressor has no effect on the odds of infection).
  • OR > 1: Higher odds of the event in the exposed group.
  • OR < 1: Lower odds of the event in the exposed group.

There are several ways to describe an OR of 1.59 in plain English:

  1. As a percentage change, using \(100 \times (OR - 1)\):

The odds of infection were 59% higher in the exposed group than in the control group.

  1. As a multiple:

The exposed group had 1.59 times the odds of infection of the control group.

  1. From the control group’s perspective, using \(1/OR\):

The control group had 0.63 times the odds of infection of the exposed group.

When the OR is below 1, use \(100 \times (1 - OR)\) to get the percentage decrease. Avoid describing an OR as “times more likely”: that describes risk, not odds (see Choosing an effect size).

Sampling variance

The variance of lnOR is:

\[ Var(lnOR) = \frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{c} + \frac{1}{d} \tag{3}\]

Continuity correction

As Equation 1 and Equation 3 show, if any cell (\(a\), \(b\), \(c\), or \(d\)) is 0 (e.g., no individuals were infected in the control group), lnOR and its variance can’t be calculated. By default, escalc() adds 0.5 to every cell of any table that contains a 0:

escalc(measure = "OR",
       ai = 10, bi = 10,
       ci = 0, di = 5)

      yi     vi 
1 2.3979 2.3723

If there are no events (or only events) in both groups, the study gives no information about the OR and should be left out (higgins2024?). You can do this with drop00 = TRUE in escalc().

Log risk ratio (lnRR)

The risk ratio (RR), or relative risk, compares the risk of the event in the exposed group with the risk in the control group. Risk is the proportion of individuals that experience the event:

\[ \begin{aligned} &n_1 = a + b\\ &n_2 = c + d\\ &RR = \frac{a/n_1}{c/n_2} \end{aligned} \tag{4}\]

An RR of 1 means the risk is the same in both groups. Because a risk can’t exceed 100%, the RR has a ceiling set by the control group risk (CGR): the RR can’t be greater than \(1/CGR\). For example, if 50% of control individuals die, the exposed group can be at most twice as likely to die, so the RR can’t exceed 2. We return to why this matters in Choosing an effect size.

As with the OR, the RR is log transformed for meta-analysis so that it is centered on 0 and approximately normal:

\[ lnRR = \ln(RR) \tag{5}\]

Calculating lnRR

dat_rr <- escalc(measure = "RR", 
                 ai = ai, bi = bi, 
                 ci = ci, di = di, 
                 data = dat_dichotomous)
dat_rr

       site ai bi ci di     yi     vi 
1 Wetland A 15 85 10 90 0.4055 0.1467 
2 Wetland B 20 30 15 35 0.2877 0.0767

Interpreting lnRR

As with lnOR, exponentiate lnRR (\(e^{lnRR}\)) to convert it back to an RR. For Wetland A:

rr_a <- exp(dat_rr$yi[1])
rr_a
[1] 1.5
  • RR = 1: Equal risk of the event in both groups (e.g., the stressor has no effect on the risk of infection).
  • RR > 1: Higher risk of the event in the exposed group.
  • RR < 1: Lower risk of the event in the exposed group.

There are two ways to describe an RR of 1.5 in plain English:

  1. As a percentage change, using \(100 \times (RR - 1)\):

Infection was 50% more likely in the exposed group than in the control group.

When the RR is below 1, use \(100 \times (1 - RR)\) to get the percentage decrease. This is called the relative risk reduction.

  1. As a multiple:

The exposed group was 1.5 times as likely to be infected as the control group.

Notice that the OR for Wetland A (1.59) is further from 1 than the RR (1.5). This gap grows as the event becomes more common.

Sampling variance

The variance of lnRR is:

\[ Var(lnRR) = \frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{a + b} + \frac{1}{c} - \frac{1}{c + d} \tag{6}\]

Continuity correction

lnRR can’t be calculated when \(a\) or \(c\) is 0. As with lnOR, escalc() adds 0.5 to every cell of any table that contains a 0 by default.

Risk difference (RD)

The risk difference (RD) is the difference in risk between the exposed and control groups. Where lnOR and lnRR describe relative change, the RD describes absolute change: how many more (or fewer) individuals experience the event per 100. This makes it useful for weighing the ecological benefits of an intervention against its costs (see Example 3 below).

\[ \begin{aligned} &n_1 = a + b\\ &n_2 = c + d\\ &RD = \frac{a}{n_1} - \frac{c}{n_2} \end{aligned} \tag{7}\]

Unlike the OR and RR, the RD is not log transformed. It already ranges from −1 to 1 and is centered on 0.

Calculating RD

dat_rd <- escalc(measure = "RD", 
                 ai = ai, bi = bi, 
                 ci = ci, di = di, 
                 data = dat_dichotomous)
dat_rd

       site ai bi ci di     yi     vi 
1 Wetland A 15 85 10 90 0.0500 0.0022 
2 Wetland B 20 30 15 35 0.1000 0.0090

Interpreting RD

  • RD = 0: Equal risk of the event in both groups.
  • RD > 0: Higher risk of the event in the exposed group.
  • RD < 0: Lower risk of the event in the exposed group (also called the absolute risk reduction).
rd_a <- dat_rd$yi[1]

In plain English, the RD of 0.05 for Wetland A can be written as:

Infection was 5 percentage points higher in the exposed group than in the control group (5 more infected individuals per 100).

Note the difference from the RR: infection was 50% more likely in the exposed group (relative change), but only 5 percentage points higher (absolute change).

Sampling variance

Because the RD is not log transformed, its variance is calculated on the raw scale:

\[ Var(RD) = \frac{a \times b}{(a + b)^3} + \frac{c \times d}{(c + d)^3} \tag{8}\]

ImportantDefining the target event

You must clearly define your target event. It is easy to accidentally swap success with failure, or fledged with failed. Switching the target event (analyzing non-events instead of events) affects each effect size differently:

  • lnOR: The sign flips (the OR becomes its reciprocal, \(1/OR\)).
  • RD: The sign flips.
  • lnRR: The estimate, its precision, and its statistical significance can all change, in ways that are hard to predict (higgins2024?).

Best practice: Define the less common outcome as the target event. This reduces errors and heterogeneity between studies (higgins2024?). Give your columns informative names to reduce data entry errors. We also recommend color-coding your columns in your spreadsheet to reduce confusion.

Choosing an effect size

Start with your study design. If studies sampled by outcome (like the beetle example below), only the OR can be calculated, so the choice is made for you. If you can calculate risks, all three effect sizes are possible, and the choice comes down to a trade-off between statistical properties and interpretation.

The case for lnOR

  • It has no ceiling. The RR is capped by the control group risk: if 50% of controls die, the exposed group can be at most twice as likely to die (RR ≤ 2). This means the same RR can be possible in one study and impossible in another with a higher baseline risk. The OR has no such cap.
  • It is symmetric. If you swap the target event (e.g., survival instead of mortality), lnOR simply changes sign. lnRR changes in value, precision, and sometimes significance (see Defining the target event above).
  • It matches logistic regression. Many studies report logistic regression coefficients, which are lnORs, so these can be combined directly with 2×2 data.

The case for lnRR

  • It is easier to understand. “1.5 times as likely” is clearer to most readers than “1.5 times the odds”.
  • It is not exaggerated when events are common. When events are common (above about 10%), the OR is further from 1 than the RR, and readers often misread an OR as an RR, overstating the effect.

The case for RD

  • It shows practical impact. “15 more patches colonized per 100” is what a manager needs to judge whether an intervention is worth its cost.
  • But it is less consistent across studies. The RD depends heavily on baseline risk, which often differs between sites and populations. It can also give impossible values when applied to other populations: an RD of −0.10 applied to a population with a 7% baseline risk predicts a risk of −3% (higgins2024?).

When events are rare (below about 10%), the OR and RR are nearly identical, and the choice between them matters little.

Our recommendation: Run the meta-analysis on lnOR, then convert the pooled result to an RR and RD for reporting, using a realistic control group risk (CGR):

\[ \begin{aligned} &RR = \frac{OR}{1 - CGR + (CGR \times OR)}\\ &RD = CGR \times (RR - 1) \end{aligned} \]

To check that your conclusions don’t depend on this choice, run the analysis with both lnOR and lnRR and report whether they agree.

NoteRare events

When events are very rare, the standard approach (calculating effect sizes with escalc() and pooling them with rma()) can give biased results. The continuity corrections and variance formulas it relies on were designed for larger counts. Based on simulation studies summarized in the Cochrane Handbook:

  • Event risks below 1%: Use the Peto odds ratio (rma.peto()), provided group sizes are similar within studies and effects are not very large.
  • Event risks above 1%, very large effects, or very unequal group sizes: Use the Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio (rma.mh(), which needs no continuity correction) or a logistic regression model (rma.glmm()).

Peto and Mantel–Haenszel are common-effect methods, so they don’t estimate heterogeneity. Cochrane’s view is that with sparse data, finding out whether there is any effect at all matters more than modeling heterogeneity. rma.glmm() can include random effects if you need them.

escalc(measure = "PETO") calculates a Peto odds ratio for each study. Pooling these with a common-effect model (rma(yi, vi, method = "EE")) gives the same result as rma.peto(). A random-effects model (the rma() default) does not, so it is not the Peto method Cochrane recommends.

Applied ecological examples: matching the effect size to your study design

Which effect size you use depends on two things: what your study design lets you calculate, and what you want to say about the result.

Example 1: You can’t calculate the proportion of each group that experienced the event → OR only

Example: Testing whether large horns protect beetles from bird predation. Researchers collect the remains of killed beetles (cases) and sample living beetles from the same area (controls), then compare how common large horns are in each group.

Why only the OR?

The researchers choose how many killed and living beetles to collect. If they collect 100 of each, half the sample is “killed”, whatever the real predation rate is. So the data can’t tell us the risk of being killed, and the RR and RD can’t be calculated. The OR still works because it gives the same answer whether you compare horn size between killed and living beetles, or compare survival between large- and small-horned beetles.

Example 2: You can calculate the proportions and want to say how many times higher or lower the risk is → RR

Example: A two-year experiment tracking 1,000 oak seedlings inside deer exclosures and 1,000 in open plots, with mortality as the target event.

Why the RR?

Every seedling is counted at planting, so we know the true risk of dying in each treatment. If 35% die in open plots and 14% in exclosures, the RR is 0.40: exclosures cut mortality risk by 60%. The OR for the same data is 0.30, which readers could mistake for a 70% cut. When an event is common, the OR makes the effect look bigger than it is.

Example 3: You can calculate the proportions and want to say how many more or fewer events occurred → RD alongside RR

Example: Comparing butterfly colonization between isolated habitat fragments and fragments linked by a corridor: 20% of isolated and 35% of connected patches are colonized over the study period.

Why add the RD?

The RR (1.75) says connected patches are 75% more likely to be colonized. The RD (0.15) says how many extra patches that is: about 15 per 100 connected, or one extra colonization for roughly every 7 patches connected. The same RR could mean a much smaller gain if colonization were rarer (4% vs. 7% is still an RR of 1.75, but only 3 extra patches per 100). The RD is what you need to judge whether corridors are worth the cost.

Comparing the three effect sizes

Effect size What it compares Scale Main strength Main limitation
OR Odds between groups Relative Works for case-control designs and matches logistic regression Less intuitive and can exaggerate relative effects for common events
RR Risk between groups Relative Direct and intuitive interpretation Requires known risks and is capped by baseline risk
RD Difference in risk Absolute Directly communicates ecological impact Less comparable across populations with different baseline risks

In short:

  • OR vs. RR: Both describe relative effects, but the OR is based on odds and the RR on risk. They are approximately equal only when the event is rare.
  • RR vs. RD: Both are based on risk, but the RR describes proportional change while the RD describes absolute change.
  • OR vs. RD: The OR describes relative change on the odds scale, whereas the RD describes absolute change. The same OR can produce very different RDs depending on baseline risk.

Multiple events per individual

lnOR, lnRR, and RD are for events that can happen only once to each individual. You can only die once.

Some events can happen to the same individual many times: infections, fires in a plot, breeding attempts. For these, the data are the number of events relative to how long individuals were monitored. These are called count or person-time data (a term borrowed from medicine; here the “persons” might be birds or vegetation plots).

There is no direct odds ratio for repeated events, because odds need a count of individuals that did and did not experience the event. You could record whether each individual had the event at least once, but this throws away information and depends on how long individuals were monitored. Count data should not be analyzed with lnOR, lnRR, or RD (higgins2024?).

escalc() offers three effect sizes for these data:

  • Log incidence rate ratio (measure = "IRR"): the relative difference in event rates. This is the most commonly used (higgins2024?).
  • Incidence rate difference (measure = "IRD"): the absolute difference in event rates.
  • Square root transformed incidence rate difference (measure = "IRSD"): a transformed version of the IRD with more stable variance.

The same reasons we prefer ratio measures for 2×2 data apply here, so we recommend the IRR, with the IRD to describe absolute impact.

All three need the number of events in each group (x1i, x2i) and the total person-time in each group (t1i, t2i), which is the total time monitored across all individuals. The data might look like this:

number_events number_individuals_monitored total_years_monitored treatment
5 15 5 experiment
15 15 5 control

First, calculate total person-time:

dat <- dat %>% 
  mutate(total_person_time = number_individuals_monitored * total_years_monitored)
knitr::kable(dat)
number_events number_individuals_monitored total_years_monitored treatment total_person_time
5 15 5 experiment 75
15 15 5 control 75

Make sure time units are the same across studies (e.g., convert months to years) before calculating.

Then make the data wide:

dat_wide <- dat %>%
  select(treatment, number_events, total_person_time) %>%
  pivot_wider(names_from = "treatment",
              values_from = c("number_events", "total_person_time"))
  
knitr::kable(dat_wide)
number_events_experiment number_events_control total_person_time_experiment total_person_time_control
5 15 75 75

Then calculate the log incidence rate ratio with escalc():

out <- escalc(measure = "IRR",
              data = dat_wide,
              x1i = number_events_experiment, x2i = number_events_control,
              t1i = total_person_time_experiment, t2i = total_person_time_control)

knitr::kable(out)
number_events_experiment number_events_control total_person_time_experiment total_person_time_control yi vi
5 15 75 75 -1.098612 0.2666667

These effect sizes are less common in ecology and evolution, so we don’t cover them further here.

Time-to-event data

Sometimes what matters is not whether an event happened, but when. How long until a radio-collared deer dies? How many days until a nest is predated? How long until a seed germinates?

These are time-to-event data (also called survival data). Each individual contributes two pieces of information: how long it was followed, and whether the event happened by the end of that time.

The complication is censoring. Some individuals are followed without the event happening: the study ends, a collar fails, or an animal leaves the study area. We know they survived at least that long, but not how long they would have survived. These individuals still carry useful information, so they shouldn’t be thrown out.

What time-to-event data look like

A 2×2 table only records whether each individual had the event. Time-to-event data also record when. At the individual level, each row is one individual, with how long it was followed and whether the event happened:

Nest Group Days followed Event
1 Fenced 12 1 (predated)
2 Fenced 30 0 (survived to end of study)
3 Unfenced 5 1 (predated)
4 Unfenced 18 0 (nest lost track of)

Rows with a 0 are censored: we know the nest survived at least that long, but not what happened after.

Summed by group, the closest match to the 2×2 table at the top of this page is:

Events Censored Total Total time at risk
Exposed (e.g., Fenced) a b a + b \(T_1\)
Control (e.g., Unfenced) c d c + d \(T_2\)

The new column, total time at risk, is the days followed added up across all individuals in the group. Dividing events by time at risk gives an event rate for each group (\(a/T_1\) and \(c/T_2\)), and the ratio of the two rates estimates the hazard ratio (introduced below):

\[ HR \approx \frac{a / T_1}{c / T_2} \]

This is the same calculation as the incidence rate ratio in Multiple events per individual, so you can use escalc(measure = "IRR") with x1i = a, x2i = c, t1i = T1, and t2i = T2.

The catch is that this only gives the true HR if the hazard stays roughly constant over the study. If risk changes over time (e.g., nest predation peaks at hatching), the summary table loses that information, and an HR from a Cox model or log-rank test will be more accurate. Papers also rarely report total time at risk, so in practice you’ll usually extract a reported HR instead (see Getting hazard ratios from studies).

ImportantDon’t use mean time-to-event

It can be tempting to compare average survival time between groups, as you would for any continuous outcome (e.g., with lnRR or SMD). Don’t. You can only measure survival time for individuals that had the event during the study. Those still alive at the end don’t have a death date, so they get left out. Leaving them out skews the average, because the individuals left out are the ones that lived longest.

For example, if a study ends after 30 days, every animal that survived past day 30 is missing from the average. The group that survived best loses the most animals from the calculation, so the comparison can make it look worse than it is.

The hazard ratio

The standard effect size for time-to-event data is the hazard ratio (HR). The hazard is the risk of the event happening at a given moment, among individuals that haven’t experienced it yet. The HR compares this between groups:

  • HR = 1: The event happens at the same rate in both groups.
  • HR > 1: The event happens sooner (or more often at any given time) in the exposed group.
  • HR < 1: The event happens later (or less often at any given time) in the exposed group.

Most analyses assume the HR stays constant over the study period, even if the hazard itself rises and falls (e.g., nest predation peaks at hatching). This is called the proportional hazards assumption. If the effect clearly changes over time (e.g., a treatment protects seedlings in the first month but not after), a single HR can be misleading.

As with the OR and RR, HRs are log transformed for meta-analysis.

In plain English, an HR of 0.60 for nest predation in fenced vs. unfenced plots means:

At any point during the nesting period, nests in fenced plots were predated at 60% of the rate of nests in unfenced plots (a 40% lower hazard).

Note that this is not the same as “40% fewer nests were predated”. The HR describes the rate at which events happen, not the final proportion.

Getting hazard ratios from studies

escalc() can’t calculate HRs from summary data, because an HR needs the individual survival times. The ideal is to obtain the raw data from study authors and reanalyze it yourself, so every study is analyzed the same way. In practice, you will usually have to work from what the papers report, which often isn’t the statistic you need (higgins2024?).

There are three ways to get an HR from a published study. For practical, step-by-step guidance on all three, see (tierney2007?).

1. Extract the reported HR and its uncertainty. Studies that use a Cox proportional hazards model (or another survival regression) report an HR, usually with a 95% CI. You can convert these to lnHR and its standard error:

\[ \begin{aligned} &lnHR = \ln(HR) \\ &SE(lnHR) = \frac{\ln(CI_{upper}) - \ln(CI_{lower})}{3.92} \end{aligned} \]

# Hazard ratios for nest predation (fenced vs. unfenced) from two studies
dat_hr <- data.frame(
  study = c("Study A", "Study B"),
  hr = c(0.62, 0.80),
  ci_lb = c(0.41, 0.55),
  ci_ub = c(0.94, 1.16)
)

dat_hr <- dat_hr %>%
  mutate(yi = log(hr),
         sei = (log(ci_ub) - log(ci_lb)) / (2 * qnorm(0.975)),
         vi = sei^2)

knitr::kable(dat_hr, digits = 3)
study hr ci_lb ci_ub yi sei vi
Study A 0.62 0.41 0.94 -0.478 0.212 0.045
Study B 0.80 0.55 1.16 -0.223 0.190 0.036 
# Meta-analysis on the log scale
res_hr <- rma(yi, vi, data = dat_hr)
res_hr

If a study reports an HR with an exact P value but no CI, you can recover the standard error from the P value (higgins2024?):

\[ SE(lnHR) = \frac{|lnHR|}{z}, \qquad z = \Phi^{-1}(1 - P/2) \]

# e.g., HR = 0.70, P = 0.03
abs(log(0.70)) / qnorm(1 - 0.03 / 2)
[1] 0.1643595

This only works with exact P values. “P < 0.05” or “NS” doesn’t give you enough information.

2. Estimate it from a log-rank test. Many studies compare survival curves with a log-rank test rather than a Cox model. If you know the observed number of events in the exposed group (O), the number expected if the exposure had no effect (E), and the variance of the log-rank statistic (V), then (simmonds2011?):

\[ lnHR \approx \frac{O - E}{V}, \qquad SE(lnHR) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{V}} \]

This is the same calculation as the Peto odds ratio, applied to survival data. Studies rarely report O, E, and V directly, but they can often be worked out from the reported P value, the number of events in each group, and the survival curves (parmar1998?; williamson2002?). These methods involve several assumptions, so it’s worth involving a statistician.

The same calculation can be done on grouped data. For example, a nest study might report how many nests were predated each week and how many were still active at the end of each week. A log-rank analysis of these counts gives O, E, and V, but because the data are grouped, the HR is only approximate (this grouped estimate is sometimes called a log odds ratio in older literature, e.g., (ebctcg1990?)). Censored individuals need careful handling, and when the intervals are long relative to the study (e.g., yearly checks in a three-year study), methods for interval-censored survival are more appropriate (collett1994?).

3. Reconstruct it from a Kaplan–Meier curve. If a study only shows a survival curve, you can digitize the curve and reconstruct approximate individual data (guyot2012?). This lets you estimate the HR yourself, and also opens up other analyses of the survival data. It takes more work, so it’s best kept for studies that can’t be included any other way.

Converting to a 2×2 table instead

If every individual was followed for at least a fixed time (say 30 days), and studies report how many in each group had experienced the event by then, you can build a 2×2 table and use lnOR, lnRR, or RD as usual. This only works if no individuals were censored before that time point. It also throws away information about when events happened, so it’s best used when most studies report outcomes this way.

Don’t mix HRs with ORs or RRs in the same meta-analysis. They measure different things and only give similar values when events are rare.

Sometimes the world is just black and white. Individuals live or die. Organisms are present or absent. Vegetation plots burn or don't burn.

Calculating effect sizes for dichotomous data starts with organizing the counts into a 2×2 table. In ecology, this usually compares an exposed (or managed) group with a control group, across an outcome with two states (e.g., mortality vs. survival, or presence vs. absence):

|                                          | **Target Event (e.g., Mortality)** | **Non-Event (e.g., Survival)** | **Total** |
| ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | --------- |
| **Exposed (e.g., Pollutant / Stressor)** | a                                  | b                              | a + b     |
| **Control (e.g., Ambient Conditions)**   | c                                  | d                              | c + d     |

We'll use this running example throughout:

```{r}
#| eval: true
#| echo: true

# 2x2 tables for amphibian chytrid infection in two wetlands
dat_dichotomous <- data.frame(
  site = c("Wetland A", "Wetland B"),
  ai = c(15, 20), # Infected, exposed (warmed mesocosm)
  bi = c(85, 30), # Uninfected, exposed (warmed mesocosm)
  ci = c(10, 15), # Infected, control (ambient)
  di = c(90, 35)  # Uninfected, control (ambient)
)

knitr::kable(dat_dichotomous)
```

We'll cover the three main effect sizes for these data: the log odds ratio (**lnOR**), the log risk ratio (**lnRR**), and the risk difference (**RD**).

:::callout-important
If the event can happen *multiple times* to the *same* individual (e.g., the number of times a plot burns or a bird breeds), use incidence rate ratios (see [Multiple events per individual](#sec-other-binary-outcomes)). If you're interested in *when* the event happened, not just whether it did (e.g., days until nest predation), use hazard ratios (see [Time-to-event data](#sec-time-to-event)).
:::

# Log odds ratio (lnOR)

The odds ratio (OR) compares the odds of the event in the exposed group with the odds in the control group. Odds are the probability of the event happening divided by the probability of it not happening, and range from 0 to infinity. An OR of 1 means the odds are the same in both groups. Using the cells of the 2×2 table:

$$
OR = \frac{a \times d}{b \times c}
$${#eq-OR}

ORs are not analyzed directly in a meta-analysis because they are bounded at 0 and are not normally distributed. For example, an OR of 0.5 and an OR of 2 are equal and opposite effects, but they are not the same distance from 1. Log transforming fixes this: lnOR is centered on 0 and ranges from $-\infty$ to $\infty$.

$$
lnOR = \ln(OR)
$${#eq-lnOR}

## Calculating lnOR

```{r}
dat_or <- escalc(measure = "OR", 
                 ai = ai, bi = bi, 
                 ci = ci, di = di, 
                 data = dat_dichotomous)
dat_or
```

## Interpreting lnOR

To interpret lnOR (or a pooled estimate and its confidence interval), exponentiate it ($e^{lnOR}$) to convert it back to an OR. For Wetland A:

```{r}
or_a <- exp(dat_or$yi[1])
or_a
```

- **OR = 1:** Equal odds of the event in both groups (e.g., the stressor has no effect on the odds of infection).
- **OR > 1:** Higher odds of the event in the exposed group.
- **OR < 1:** Lower odds of the event in the exposed group.

There are several ways to describe an OR of `r round(or_a, 2)` in plain English:

1. As a percentage change, using $100 \times (OR - 1)$:

> The odds of infection were `r round(100 * (or_a - 1))`% higher in the exposed group than in the control group.

2. As a multiple:

> The exposed group had `r round(or_a, 2)` times the odds of infection of the control group.

3. From the control group's perspective, using $1/OR$:

> The control group had `r round(1 / or_a, 2)` times the odds of infection of the exposed group.

When the OR is below 1, use $100 \times (1 - OR)$ to get the percentage decrease. Avoid describing an OR as "times more likely": that describes risk, not odds (see [Choosing an effect size](#sec-choosing)).

## Sampling variance

The variance of lnOR is:

$$
Var(lnOR) = \frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{c} + \frac{1}{d}
$${#eq-lnOR-var}

## Continuity correction

As @eq-OR and @eq-lnOR-var show, if any cell ($a$, $b$, $c$, or $d$) is 0 (e.g., no individuals were infected in the control group), lnOR and its variance can't be calculated. By default, `escalc()` adds 0.5 to every cell of any table that contains a 0:

```{r}
escalc(measure = "OR",
       ai = 10, bi = 10,
       ci = 0, di = 5)
```

If there are no events (or only events) in *both* groups, the study gives no information about the OR and should be left out [@higgins2024]. You can do this with `drop00 = TRUE` in `escalc()`.

# Log risk ratio (lnRR)

The risk ratio (RR), or relative risk, compares the risk of the event in the exposed group with the risk in the control group. Risk is the proportion of individuals that experience the event:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&n_1 = a + b\\
&n_2 = c + d\\
&RR = \frac{a/n_1}{c/n_2}
\end{aligned}
$${#eq-RR}

An RR of 1 means the risk is the same in both groups. Because a risk can't exceed 100%, the RR has a ceiling set by the control group risk (CGR): the RR can't be greater than $1/CGR$. For example, if 50% of control individuals die, the exposed group can be at most twice as likely to die, so the RR can't exceed 2. We return to why this matters in [Choosing an effect size](#sec-choosing).

As with the OR, the RR is log transformed for meta-analysis so that it is centered on 0 and approximately normal:

$$
lnRR = \ln(RR)
$${#eq-lnRR}

## Calculating lnRR

```{r}
dat_rr <- escalc(measure = "RR", 
                 ai = ai, bi = bi, 
                 ci = ci, di = di, 
                 data = dat_dichotomous)
dat_rr
```

## Interpreting lnRR

As with lnOR, exponentiate lnRR ($e^{lnRR}$) to convert it back to an RR. For Wetland A:

```{r}
rr_a <- exp(dat_rr$yi[1])
rr_a
```

- **RR = 1:** Equal risk of the event in both groups (e.g., the stressor has no effect on the risk of infection).
- **RR > 1:** Higher risk of the event in the exposed group.
- **RR < 1:** Lower risk of the event in the exposed group.

There are two ways to describe an RR of `r round(rr_a, 2)` in plain English:

1. As a percentage change, using $100 \times (RR - 1)$:

> Infection was `r round(100 * (rr_a - 1))`% more likely in the exposed group than in the control group.

When the RR is below 1, use $100 \times (1 - RR)$ to get the percentage decrease. This is called the [relative risk reduction](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-15#section-15-4).

2. As a multiple:

> The exposed group was `r round(rr_a, 2)` times as likely to be infected as the control group.

Notice that the OR for Wetland A (`r round(or_a, 2)`) is further from 1 than the RR (`r round(rr_a, 2)`). This gap grows as the event becomes more common.

## Sampling variance

The variance of lnRR is:

$$
Var(lnRR) = \frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{a + b} + \frac{1}{c} - \frac{1}{c + d}
$${#eq-lnRR-var}

## Continuity correction

lnRR can't be calculated when $a$ or $c$ is 0. As with lnOR, `escalc()` adds 0.5 to every cell of any table that contains a 0 by default.

# Risk difference (RD)

The risk difference (RD) is the difference in risk between the exposed and control groups. Where lnOR and lnRR describe *relative* change, the RD describes *absolute* change: how many more (or fewer) individuals experience the event per 100. This makes it useful for weighing the ecological benefits of an intervention against its costs (see Example 3 below).

$$
\begin{aligned}
&n_1 = a + b\\
&n_2 = c + d\\
&RD = \frac{a}{n_1} - \frac{c}{n_2}
\end{aligned}
$${#eq-RD}

Unlike the OR and RR, the RD is not log transformed. It already ranges from −1 to 1 and is centered on 0.

## Calculating RD

```{r}
dat_rd <- escalc(measure = "RD", 
                 ai = ai, bi = bi, 
                 ci = ci, di = di, 
                 data = dat_dichotomous)
dat_rd
```

## Interpreting RD

- **RD = 0:** Equal risk of the event in both groups.
- **RD > 0:** Higher risk of the event in the exposed group.
- **RD < 0:** Lower risk of the event in the exposed group (also called the absolute risk reduction).

```{r}
rd_a <- dat_rd$yi[1]
```

In plain English, the RD of `r round(rd_a, 2)` for Wetland A can be written as:

> Infection was `r round(100 * rd_a)` percentage points higher in the exposed group than in the control group (`r round(100 * rd_a)` more infected individuals per 100).

Note the difference from the RR: infection was `r round(100 * (rr_a - 1))`% *more likely* in the exposed group (relative change), but only `r round(100 * rd_a)` *percentage points* higher (absolute change).

## Sampling variance

Because the RD is not log transformed, its variance is calculated on the raw scale:

$$
Var(RD) = \frac{a \times b}{(a + b)^3} + \frac{c \times d}{(c + d)^3}
$${#eq-RD-var}

:::callout-important
#### Defining the target event

You must clearly define your target event. It is easy to accidentally swap **success** with **failure**, or **fledged** with **failed**. Switching the target event (analyzing non-events instead of events) affects each effect size differently:

- **lnOR:** The sign flips (the OR becomes its reciprocal, $1/OR$).
- **RD:** The sign flips.
- **lnRR:** The estimate, its precision, and its statistical significance can all change, in ways that are hard to predict [@higgins2024].

*Best practice:* Define the less common outcome as the target event. This reduces errors and heterogeneity between studies [@higgins2024]. Give your columns informative names to reduce data entry errors. We also recommend color-coding your columns in your spreadsheet to reduce confusion.
:::

# Choosing an effect size {#sec-choosing}

Start with your study design. If studies sampled by outcome (like the beetle example below), only the OR can be calculated, so the choice is made for you. If you can calculate risks, all three effect sizes are possible, and the choice comes down to a trade-off between statistical properties and interpretation.

**The case for lnOR**

- **It has no ceiling.** The RR is capped by the control group risk: if 50% of controls die, the exposed group can be at most twice as likely to die (RR ≤ 2). This means the same RR can be possible in one study and impossible in another with a higher baseline risk. The OR has no such cap.
- **It is symmetric.** If you swap the target event (e.g., survival instead of mortality), lnOR simply changes sign. lnRR changes in value, precision, and sometimes significance (see *Defining the target event* above).
- **It matches logistic regression.** Many studies report logistic regression coefficients, which are lnORs, so these can be combined directly with 2×2 data.

**The case for lnRR**

- **It is easier to understand.** "1.5 times as likely" is clearer to most readers than "1.5 times the odds".
- **It is not exaggerated when events are common.** When events are common (above about 10%), the OR is further from 1 than the RR, and readers often misread an OR as an RR, overstating the effect.

**The case for RD**

- **It shows practical impact.** "15 more patches colonized per 100" is what a manager needs to judge whether an intervention is worth its cost.
- **But it is less consistent across studies.** The RD depends heavily on baseline risk, which often differs between sites and populations. It can also give impossible values when applied to other populations: an RD of −0.10 applied to a population with a 7% baseline risk predicts a risk of −3% [@higgins2024].

**When events are rare** (below about 10%), the OR and RR are nearly identical, and the choice between them matters little.

**Our recommendation:** Run the meta-analysis on lnOR, then convert the pooled result to an RR and RD for reporting, using a realistic control group risk (CGR):

$$
\begin{aligned}
&RR = \frac{OR}{1 - CGR + (CGR \times OR)}\\
&RD = CGR \times (RR - 1)
\end{aligned}
$$

To check that your conclusions don't depend on this choice, run the analysis with both lnOR and lnRR and report whether they agree.

:::callout-note
#### Rare events

When events are very rare, the standard approach (calculating effect sizes with `escalc()` and pooling them with `rma()`) can give biased results. The continuity corrections and variance formulas it relies on were designed for larger counts. Based on simulation studies summarized in the [Cochrane Handbook](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-10#section-10-4-3):

- **Event risks below 1%:** Use the Peto odds ratio (`rma.peto()`), provided group sizes are similar within studies and effects are not very large.
- **Event risks above 1%, very large effects, or very unequal group sizes:** Use the Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio (`rma.mh()`, which needs no continuity correction) or a logistic regression model (`rma.glmm()`).

Peto and Mantel–Haenszel are common-effect methods, so they don't estimate heterogeneity. Cochrane's view is that with sparse data, finding out whether there is any effect at all matters more than modeling heterogeneity. `rma.glmm()` can include random effects if you need them.

`escalc(measure = "PETO")` calculates a Peto odds ratio for each study. Pooling these with a common-effect model (`rma(yi, vi, method = "EE")`) gives the same result as `rma.peto()`. A random-effects model (the `rma()` default) does not, so it is not the Peto method Cochrane recommends.
:::

:::callout-note
#### Paired or longitudinal outcome data

Is this provided by the matched pairs contingency tables in `escalc`? If so, can they be compared to unpaired estimators or does the entire meta-analysis need to focus on these alone?
:::

## Applied ecological examples: matching the effect size to your study design

Which effect size you use depends on two things: what your study design lets you calculate, and what you want to say about the result.

**Example 1: You can't calculate the proportion of each group that experienced the event → OR only**

*Example:* Testing whether large horns protect beetles from bird predation. Researchers collect the remains of killed beetles (cases) and sample living beetles from the same area (controls), then compare how common large horns are in each group.

**Why only the OR?**

The researchers choose how many killed and living beetles to collect. If they collect 100 of each, half the sample is "killed", whatever the real predation rate is. So the data can't tell us the risk of being killed, and the RR and RD can't be calculated. The OR still works because it gives the same answer whether you compare horn size between killed and living beetles, or compare survival between large- and small-horned beetles.

**Example 2: You can calculate the proportions and want to say how many times higher or lower the risk is → RR**

*Example:* A two-year experiment tracking 1,000 oak seedlings inside deer exclosures and 1,000 in open plots, with mortality as the target event.

**Why the RR?**

Every seedling is counted at planting, so we know the true risk of dying in each treatment. If 35% die in open plots and 14% in exclosures, the RR is 0.40: exclosures cut mortality risk by 60%. The OR for the same data is 0.30, which readers could mistake for a 70% cut. When an event is common, the OR makes the effect look bigger than it is.

**Example 3: You can calculate the proportions and want to say how many more or fewer events occurred → RD alongside RR**

*Example:* Comparing butterfly colonization between isolated habitat fragments and fragments linked by a corridor: 20% of isolated and 35% of connected patches are colonized over the study period.

**Why add the RD?**

The RR (1.75) says connected patches are 75% more likely to be colonized. The RD (0.15) says how many extra patches that is: about 15 per 100 connected, or one extra colonization for roughly every 7 patches connected. The same RR could mean a much smaller gain if colonization were rarer (4% vs. 7% is still an RR of 1.75, but only 3 extra patches per 100). The RD is what you need to judge whether corridors are worth the cost.

## Comparing the three effect sizes

| Effect size | What it compares | Scale | Main strength | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **OR** | Odds between groups | Relative | Works for case-control designs and matches logistic regression | Less intuitive and can exaggerate relative effects for common events |
| **RR** | Risk between groups | Relative | Direct and intuitive interpretation | Requires known risks and is capped by baseline risk |
| **RD** | Difference in risk | Absolute | Directly communicates ecological impact | Less comparable across populations with different baseline risks |

In short:

- **OR vs. RR:** Both describe relative effects, but the OR is based on odds and the RR on risk. They are approximately equal only when the event is rare.
- **RR vs. RD:** Both are based on risk, but the RR describes proportional change while the RD describes absolute change.
- **OR vs. RD:** The OR describes relative change on the odds scale, whereas the RD describes absolute change. The same OR can produce very different RDs depending on baseline risk.

# Multiple events per individual {#sec-other-binary-outcomes}

lnOR, lnRR, and RD are for events that can happen only once to each individual. You can only die once.

Some events can happen to the same individual many times: infections, fires in a plot, breeding attempts. For these, the data are the number of events relative to how long individuals were monitored. These are called count or person-time data (a term borrowed from medicine; here the "persons" might be birds or vegetation plots).

There is no direct odds ratio for repeated events, because odds need a count of individuals that did and did not experience the event. You could record whether each individual had the event at least once, but this throws away information and depends on how long individuals were monitored. Count data should not be analyzed with lnOR, lnRR, or RD [@higgins2024].

`escalc()` offers three effect sizes for these data:

- **Log incidence rate ratio** (`measure = "IRR"`): the relative difference in event rates. This is the most commonly used [@higgins2024].
- **Incidence rate difference** (`measure = "IRD"`): the absolute difference in event rates.
- **Square root transformed incidence rate difference** (`measure = "IRSD"`): a transformed version of the IRD with more stable variance.

The same reasons we prefer ratio measures for 2×2 data apply here, so we recommend the IRR, with the IRD to describe absolute impact.

All three need the number of events in each group (`x1i`, `x2i`) and the total person-time in each group (`t1i`, `t2i`), which is the total time monitored across all individuals. The data might look like this:

```{r}
#| echo: false
dat <- data.frame(number_events = c(5, 15),
                  number_individuals_monitored = c(15, 15),
                  total_years_monitored = c(5, 5),
                  treatment = c("experiment", "control"))
knitr::kable(dat)
```

First, calculate total person-time:

```{r}
dat <- dat %>% 
  mutate(total_person_time = number_individuals_monitored * total_years_monitored)
knitr::kable(dat)
```

Make sure time units are the same across studies (e.g., convert months to years) before calculating.

Then make the data wide:

```{r}
dat_wide <- dat %>%
  select(treatment, number_events, total_person_time) %>%
  pivot_wider(names_from = "treatment",
              values_from = c("number_events", "total_person_time"))
  
knitr::kable(dat_wide)
```

Then calculate the log incidence rate ratio with `escalc()`:

```{r}
out <- escalc(measure = "IRR",
              data = dat_wide,
              x1i = number_events_experiment, x2i = number_events_control,
              t1i = total_person_time_experiment, t2i = total_person_time_control)

knitr::kable(out)
```

These effect sizes are less common in ecology and evolution, so we don't cover them further here.

# Time-to-event data {#sec-time-to-event}

Sometimes what matters is not *whether* an event happened, but *when*. How long until a radio-collared deer dies? How many days until a nest is predated? How long until a seed germinates?

These are time-to-event data (also called survival data). Each individual contributes two pieces of information: how long it was followed, and whether the event happened by the end of that time.

The complication is **censoring**. Some individuals are followed without the event happening: the study ends, a collar fails, or an animal leaves the study area. We know they survived *at least* that long, but not how long they would have survived. These individuals still carry useful information, so they shouldn't be thrown out.

## What time-to-event data look like

A 2×2 table only records *whether* each individual had the event. Time-to-event data also record *when*. At the individual level, each row is one individual, with how long it was followed and whether the event happened:

| **Nest** | **Group** | **Days followed** | **Event** |
| -------- | --------- | ----------------- | --------- |
| 1 | Fenced | 12 | 1 (predated) |
| 2 | Fenced | 30 | 0 (survived to end of study) |
| 3 | Unfenced | 5 | 1 (predated) |
| 4 | Unfenced | 18 | 0 (nest lost track of) |

Rows with a 0 are censored: we know the nest survived at least that long, but not what happened after.

Summed by group, the closest match to the 2×2 table at the top of this page is:

|                                          | **Events** | **Censored** | **Total** | **Total time at risk** |
| ---------------------------------------- | ---------- | ------------ | --------- | ---------------------- |
| **Exposed (e.g., Fenced)**               | a          | b            | a + b     | $T_1$                  |
| **Control (e.g., Unfenced)**             | c          | d            | c + d     | $T_2$                  |

The new column, total time at risk, is the days followed added up across all individuals in the group. Dividing events by time at risk gives an event rate for each group ($a/T_1$ and $c/T_2$), and the ratio of the two rates estimates the hazard ratio (introduced below):

$$
HR \approx \frac{a / T_1}{c / T_2}
$$

This is the same calculation as the incidence rate ratio in [Multiple events per individual](#sec-other-binary-outcomes), so you can use `escalc(measure = "IRR")` with `x1i = a`, `x2i = c`, `t1i = T1`, and `t2i = T2`.

The catch is that this only gives the true HR if the hazard stays roughly constant over the study. If risk changes over time (e.g., nest predation peaks at hatching), the summary table loses that information, and an HR from a Cox model or log-rank test will be more accurate. Papers also rarely report total time at risk, so in practice you'll usually extract a reported HR instead (see [Getting hazard ratios from studies](#sec-getting-hr)).

:::callout-important
#### Don't use mean time-to-event

It can be tempting to compare average survival time between groups, as you would for any continuous outcome (e.g., with lnRR or SMD). Don't. You can only measure survival time for individuals that had the event during the study. Those still alive at the end don't have a death date, so they get left out. Leaving them out skews the average, because the individuals left out are the ones that lived longest.

For example, if a study ends after 30 days, every animal that survived past day 30 is missing from the average. The group that survived best loses the most animals from the calculation, so the comparison can make it look worse than it is.
:::

## The hazard ratio

The standard effect size for time-to-event data is the **hazard ratio (HR)**. The hazard is the risk of the event happening at a given moment, among individuals that haven't experienced it yet. The HR compares this between groups:

- **HR = 1:** The event happens at the same rate in both groups.
- **HR > 1:** The event happens sooner (or more often at any given time) in the exposed group.
- **HR < 1:** The event happens later (or less often at any given time) in the exposed group.

Most analyses assume the HR stays constant over the study period, even if the hazard itself rises and falls (e.g., nest predation peaks at hatching). This is called the **proportional hazards assumption**. If the effect clearly changes over time (e.g., a treatment protects seedlings in the first month but not after), a single HR can be misleading.

As with the OR and RR, HRs are log transformed for meta-analysis.

In plain English, an HR of 0.60 for nest predation in fenced vs. unfenced plots means:

> At any point during the nesting period, nests in fenced plots were predated at 60% of the rate of nests in unfenced plots (a 40% lower hazard).

Note that this is *not* the same as "40% fewer nests were predated". The HR describes the rate at which events happen, not the final proportion.

## Getting hazard ratios from studies {#sec-getting-hr}

`escalc()` can't calculate HRs from summary data, because an HR needs the individual survival times. The ideal is to obtain the raw data from study authors and reanalyze it yourself, so every study is analyzed the same way. In practice, you will usually have to work from what the papers report, which often isn't the statistic you need [@higgins2024].

There are three ways to get an HR from a published study. For practical, step-by-step guidance on all three, see @tierney2007.

**1. Extract the reported HR and its uncertainty.** Studies that use a Cox proportional hazards model (or another survival regression) report an HR, usually with a 95% CI. You can convert these to lnHR and its standard error:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&lnHR = \ln(HR) \\
&SE(lnHR) = \frac{\ln(CI_{upper}) - \ln(CI_{lower})}{3.92}
\end{aligned}
$$

```{r}
# Hazard ratios for nest predation (fenced vs. unfenced) from two studies
dat_hr <- data.frame(
  study = c("Study A", "Study B"),
  hr = c(0.62, 0.80),
  ci_lb = c(0.41, 0.55),
  ci_ub = c(0.94, 1.16)
)

dat_hr <- dat_hr %>%
  mutate(yi = log(hr),
         sei = (log(ci_ub) - log(ci_lb)) / (2 * qnorm(0.975)),
         vi = sei^2)

knitr::kable(dat_hr, digits = 3)

# Meta-analysis on the log scale
res_hr <- rma(yi, vi, data = dat_hr)
res_hr

# Back-transform to an HR
predict(res_hr, transf = exp)
```

If a study reports an HR with an exact P value but no CI, you can recover the standard error from the P value [@higgins2024]:

$$
SE(lnHR) = \frac{|lnHR|}{z}, \qquad z = \Phi^{-1}(1 - P/2)
$$

```{r}
# e.g., HR = 0.70, P = 0.03
abs(log(0.70)) / qnorm(1 - 0.03 / 2)
```

This only works with exact P values. "P < 0.05" or "NS" doesn't give you enough information.

**2. Estimate it from a log-rank test.** Many studies compare survival curves with a log-rank test rather than a Cox model. If you know the observed number of events in the exposed group (O), the number expected if the exposure had no effect (E), and the variance of the log-rank statistic (V), then [@simmonds2011]:

$$
lnHR \approx \frac{O - E}{V}, \qquad SE(lnHR) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}
$$

This is the same calculation as the Peto odds ratio, applied to survival data. Studies rarely report O, E, and V directly, but they can often be worked out from the reported P value, the number of events in each group, and the survival curves [@parmar1998; @williamson2002]. These methods involve several assumptions, so it's worth involving a statistician.

The same calculation can be done on grouped data. For example, a nest study might report how many nests were predated each week and how many were still active at the end of each week. A log-rank analysis of these counts gives O, E, and V, but because the data are grouped, the HR is only approximate (this grouped estimate is sometimes called a log odds ratio in older literature, e.g., @ebctcg1990). Censored individuals need careful handling, and when the intervals are long relative to the study (e.g., yearly checks in a three-year study), methods for interval-censored survival are more appropriate [@collett1994].

**3. Reconstruct it from a Kaplan–Meier curve.** If a study only shows a survival curve, you can digitize the curve and reconstruct approximate individual data [@guyot2012]. This lets you estimate the HR yourself, and also opens up other analyses of the survival data. It takes more work, so it's best kept for studies that can't be included any other way.

## Converting to a 2×2 table instead

If every individual was followed for at least a fixed time (say 30 days), and studies report how many in each group had experienced the event by then, you can build a 2×2 table and use lnOR, lnRR, or RD as usual. This only works if no individuals were censored before that time point. It also throws away information about *when* events happened, so it's best used when most studies report outcomes this way.

Don't mix HRs with ORs or RRs in the same meta-analysis. They measure different things and only give similar values when events are rare.
