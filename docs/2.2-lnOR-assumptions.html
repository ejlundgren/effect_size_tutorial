Effect Sizes for Dichotomous Data: Assumptions and Interpretation

Odds Ratio, Risk Ratio, Risk Difference, and Hazard Ratio

Published

October 30, 2026

TipKey points
  • lnOR (log odds ratio) and lnRR (log risk ratios) describe discrete binary outcomes between two groups (i.e., experiment vs control)
  • Odds ratios (OR) compare odds of a binary outcome occurring between two groups
  • Risk ratios (RR) compare risks of the outcome. The two are close only when the event is rare.
  • Odds are not risk. The same OR can mean a small or a large change in risk, depending on how common the event is in the control group.
  • Zero cells need a continuity correction, which is applied automatically by escalc. For studies with no events in either group, we recommend drop00 = TRUE in escalc.
  • If the event in question is very rare, consider directly modeling outcomes with rma.glmm(), which does not require continuity correction.
  • Do not use lnOR or lnRR for binary outcomes that can occur multiple times per individual (or plot or site). Use incident rate ratio estimands for this type of ‘event count’ data.

Sometimes the world is just black and white. Individuals live or die. Organisms are present or absent. Vegetation plots burn or don’t burn.

Dichotomous data is often organized as the counts in a 2×2 table. In ecology, this usually compares an exposed (or managed) group with a control group, across an outcome with two states (e.g., mortality vs. survival, or presence vs. absence):

Target Event (e.g., Mortality) Non-Event (e.g., Survival) Total
Exposed (e.g., Pollutant / Stressor) a b a + b
Control (e.g., Ambient Conditions) c d c + d

We’ll use a running example throughout. Imag

# 2x2 tables for amphibian chytrid infection in two wetlands
dat_dichotomous <- data.frame(
  site = c("Wetland A", "Wetland B"),
  ai = c(15, 20), # Infected in a warmed mesocosm (exposure treatment)
  bi = c(85, 30), # Uninfected in a warmed mesocosm (exposure treatment)
  ci = c(10, 15), # Infected in the ambient mesocosm (control treatment)
  di = c(90, 35)  # Uninfected in the ambient mesocosm (control treatment)
)

knitr::kable(dat_dichotomous)
site ai bi ci di
Wetland A 15 85 10 90
Wetland B 20 30 15 35

Each experiment compares infection in warmed mesocosms (the exposed group) with ambient controls (the control group). Experiment 1 has 100 individuals in each group, and Experiment 2 has 50.

We’ll cover the two main effect sizes for these data: the log odds ratio (lnOR) and the log risk ratio (lnRR)

Important

If the event can happen multiple times to the same individual (e.g., the number of times a plot burns or a bird breeds), use incidence rate ratios (see Multiple events per individual). If you’re interested in when the event happened, not just whether it did (e.g., days until nest predation), use hazard ratios (see Time-to-event data).

Log odds ratio (lnOR)

The odds of an event are the chance that it happens divided by the chance that it does not. For example, if 20 out of 100 individuals die, the odds of death are 20 to 80, or 0.25. The odds ratio (OR) divides the odds of the event in the exposed group (group 1, the treatment) by the odds in the control group (group 2). An OR of 1 means the odds are the same in both groups.

The math is below. Here \(a\) and \(b\) are the number of individuals in the exposed group who did and did not have the event, and \(c\) and \(d\) are the same counts for the control group. In other words, \(a\) is the exposed group with the event.

\[\begin{aligned} &OR=\frac{a \times d}{b \times c} \\ \end{aligned} \tag{1}\]

ORs are not analyzed directly in a meta-analysis because they are bounded at 0 and are not normally distributed. For example, an OR of 0.5 and an OR of 2 are equal and opposite effects, but they are not the same distance from 1. Log transforming fixes this: lnOR is centered on 0 and ranges from \(-\infty\) to \(\infty\).

\[\begin{aligned} &lnOR = \ln(OR)\\ \end{aligned} \tag{2}\]

Calculating lnOR

Let’s calculate lnOR for our example data with escalc in metafor:

# Calculate lnOR for each experiment with escalc
dat_or <- escalc(measure = "OR",
                 ai = ai, bi = bi,
                 ci = ci, di = di,
                 data = dat_dichotomous)
dat_or

Interpreting lnOR

To interpret lnOR (including a pooled model estimate ± confidence intervals), exponentiate it (\(e^{lnOR}\)) to convert it back to an OR. Let’s take our

or_a <- exp(dat_or$yi[1])
round(or_a, 1)
[1] 1.6
  • OR = 1: Equal odds of the event in both groups (e.g., the stressor has no effect on the odds of infection).
  • OR > 1: Higher odds of the event in the exposed group.
  • OR < 1: Lower odds of the event in the exposed group.

There are several ways to describe an OR of 1.59 in plain English, which are more or less comfortable based on whether OR is ≥ 1 or < 1.

  1. If OR is ≥ 1, convert to percent change, using \(100 \times (OR - 1)\):

The odds of infection were 59% higher in the exposed group than in the control group.

  1. You can also write in terms of multiples by rounding the odds ratio:

The exposed group had 1.59 times the odds of infection of the control group.

  1. If the OR value is < 1, it might be easier to write from the control group’s perspective, using \(1/OR\):

The control group had 0.63 times the odds of infection than the exposed group.

  1. Similarly, when the OR is below 1, you can use \(100 \times (1 - OR)\) to get the percentage decrease.

The control group had -58.82% reduction in the odds of infection.

Avoid describing an OR as “times more likely”: that describes risk, not odds (see Choosing an effect size).

NoteClick to understand why odds are not risk

Halving the odds is not the same as halving the risk. The change in risk depends on the baseline risk, \(p_0\), which is the risk in the control group. Given an OR and a baseline risk, the risk in the exposed group is:

\[p_1 = \frac{OR \times p_0}{1 - p_0 + OR \times p_0}\]

The table below shows an OR of 0.5 at three different baseline risks:

Baseline (control) risk Risk in exposed group Risk ratio (RR)
5% 2.6% 0.51
30% 17.6% 0.59
60% 42.9% 0.71

With a 5% baseline risk, an OR of 0.5 lowers the risk from 5% to 2.6%, a relative drop of about the same size as the drop in odds. With a 60% baseline risk, the same OR only lowers the risk from 60% to 42.9%, an RR of 0.71.

Sampling variance

Variance for the OR is calculated on a natural logarithmic scale:

\[ Var(lnOR) = \frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{c} + \frac{1}{d} \tag{3}\]

Continuity correction

As you can see in Equation 2 and Equation 3, if any cell (\(a\), \(b\), \(c\), or \(d\)) is 0 (e.g., no individuals were infected in the control group), lnOR and its variance can’t be calculated (\(\frac{x}{0}=\infty\). By default, escalc() adds 0.5 to every cell of any table that contains a 0:

escalc(measure = "OR",
       ai = 10, bi = 10,
       ci = 0, di = 5)

      yi     vi 
1 2.3979 2.3723

If there are no events (or only events) in both groups, the study gives no information about the OR and should be left out (higgins2024?). You can do this by adding drop00 = TRUE in escalc() or you can manually exclude those data points.

We discuss other methods for rare events and many 0 cells below.

Log risk ratio (lnRR)

The Risk Ratio (RR), or relative risk, compares the probability (risk) of an event in the exposed group with the risk in the control group.Each risk is the number of individuals with the outcome divided by the total number of individuals in that group.

\[ \begin{aligned} &n_1 = a + b\\ &n_2 = c + d\\ &RR = \frac{a/n_1}{c/n_2} \end{aligned} \tag{4}\]

The RR is interpreted much like the OR, but it compares probabilities directly, which makes it easier to explain. It also has a ceiling. Because a risk cannot be larger than 1, \(RR = p_1/p_0\) can never be larger than \(1/p_0\), where \(p_0\) is the control risk. For example, if half of the controls experience the event, the RR cannot be larger than 2. This ceiling matters most when the event is common, and it is one reason lnRR can vary across studies with different baseline risks. The OR has no such ceiling.

As with the OR, the RR is log transformed for meta-analysis so that it is centered on 0 and approximately normal:

\[ lnRR = \ln(RR) \tag{5}\]

Calculating lnRR

dat_rr <- escalc(measure = "RR", 
                 ai = ai, bi = bi, 
                 ci = ci, di = di, 
                 data = dat_dichotomous)

Interpreting lnRR

To interpret these effect sizes (and the final model estimates), you first convert lnRR to RR by exponentiating (\(e^{lnRR}\)):

rr_a <- exp(dat_rr$yi[1])
rr_a
[1] 1.5

For Experiment 1, the lnRR is 0.405, which is an RR of 1.5.

Interpret RR as follows:

  • RR = 1: Equal risk (or probability) between the comparison groups (e.g., a stressor has no effect on overall mortality risk).

  • RR > 1: Higher risk (or probability) of the event in the exposed group.

  • RR < 1: Lower risk (or probability) of the event in the exposed group.

In plain English, you can write the RR for Experiment 1 in two ways:

There are two ways to describe an RR of 1.5 in plain English:

  1. Subtract 1 from the RR and multiply by 100 to get the relative change in risk: \(100 \times (RR - 1)\). A positive value means higher risk in the exposed group, and a negative value means lower risk. When RR is below 1, \(100 \times (1 - RR)\) is called the Relative Risk Reduction.

The outcome is 50% more likely in the exposed group than in the control group.

When the RR is below 1, use \(100 \times (1 - RR)\) to get the percentage decrease. This is called the relative risk reduction.

  1. You can also report the RR directly, which is easier to interpret than the OR.

The exposed group was 1.5 times as likely to experience the outcome as the control group.

Note

Notice that the OR for Wetland A (1.59) is further from 1 than the RR (1.5). This gap grows as the event becomes more common.

Sampling variance

Like lnOR, lnRR variance is calculated on the log scale:

\[ Var(lnRR) = \frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{a + b} + \frac{1}{c} - \frac{1}{c + d} \tag{6}\]

Continuity correction

lnRR breaks down when \(a\) or \(c\) equals 0, so it also needs a continuity correction. Like lnOR, escalc adds 0.5 to all four cells of the affected table, not only the affected group, so \(n_1\) and \(n_2\) each increase by 1. The same advice about studies with no events in either group applies here: set drop00 = TRUE unless you have a good reason to keep them.

ImportantDefining the target event

You must clearly define your target event. It is easy to accidentally swap success with failure, or fledged with failed. Switching the target event (analyzing non-events instead of events) affects each effect size differently:

  • OR: The new estimate becomes the exact reciprocal (\(1/x\)). In the 30% baseline example above, a mortality OR of 0.5 becomes a survival OR of 2.

  • RR: The estimate changes and is not simply the reciprocal. Its precision and significance can also change, sometimes substantially, which is a troubling mathematical property of RR. In the same 30% example, the mortality RR is 0.59, but the survival RR is 1.18.

Best practice: Define the less common outcome as the target event. This reduces errors and heterogeneity between studies (higgins2024?). Give your columns informative names to reduce data entry errors and utilize reproducible, trackable extraction practices, such as provided by the R package metaDigitise. We also recommend color-coding your columns in your spreadsheet to reduce confusion.

When to use lnOR or lnRR

Start with your study design. If studies sampled by outcome (like the beetle example below), only the OR can be calculated, so the choice is made for you. If you can calculate risks, all three effect sizes are possible, and the choice comes down to a trade-off between statistical properties and interpretation.

The case for lnOR

  • It has no ceiling. The RR is capped by the control group risk: if 50% of controls die, the exposed group can be at most twice as likely to die (RR ≤ 2). This means the same RR can be possible in one study and impossible in another with a higher baseline risk. The OR has no such cap.
  • It is symmetric. If you swap the target event (e.g., survival instead of mortality), lnOR simply changes sign. lnRR changes in value, precision, and sometimes significance (see Defining the target event above).
  • It matches logistic regression. Many studies report logistic regression coefficients, which are lnORs, so these can be combined directly with 2×2 data.

The case for lnRR

  • It is easier to understand. “1.5 times as likely” is clearer to most readers than “1.5 times the odds”.
  • It is not exaggerated when events are common. When events are common (above about 10%), the OR is further from 1 than the RR, and readers often misread an OR as an RR, overstating the effect.

When events are rare (below about 10%), the OR and RR are nearly identical, and the choice between them matters little. However, continuity corrections can bias your results. See below for discussion of other approaches.

Our recommendation: Run the meta-analysis on lnOR and lnRR to ensure that your results don’t depend on this choice. If your estimates are similar, then convert the lnRR results to plain english for easier interpretability.

NoteRare events

When events are very rare, the standard approach (calculating effect sizes with escalc() and pooling them with rma.v()) can give biased results. The continuity corrections and variance formulas it relies on were designed for larger counts. Based on simulation studies summarized in the Cochrane Handbook:

  • Event risks below 1%: If the group sizes are similar within studies and effects are not very large, use Peto’s odds ratio. This method can be applied either by calculating odds ratio effect sizes with the PETO method (escalc(measure = "PETO")) and analyzing with rma(yi, vi, method = "EE") or inputting your cell counts (ai, bi, ci, di) directly into the model with rma.peto(), which will produce identical results.

  • Event risks above 1%, very large effects, or unequal group sizes: Use the Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio (rma.mh(), which needs no continuity correction) or a logistic regression model (rma.glmm()). As with rma.peto(), you submit your cell counts directly to rma.glmm(). This method directly models the outcomes without applying continuity corrections:

res.glmm <- rma.glmm(ai=ai, bi=bi, ci=ci, di=di, 
                     measure="OR", 
                     data=dat)

Peto and Mantel–Haenszel are common-effect methods, so they don’t estimate heterogeneity and don’t allow you to control for non-independence structures in your data. Cochrane’s view is that with sparse data, finding out whether there is any effect at all matters more than modeling heterogeneity.

However, in ecology, where heterogeneity is king, we recommend using rma.glmm() in most cases, which can handle random effects.

If you are dealing with very rare events (<1%), we recommend doing both rma.peto() and rma.glmm() and reporting the results of both approaches.

Applied ecological examples: matching the effect size to study design

The discussion so far has concerned the interpretatibility of lnOR and lnRR but the primary study designs you encounter add further limitations. We’ll give a couple examples to help think this through.

Example 1

You cannot calculate the proportion of each group that experienced the event → OR only

Example: Testing whether horns deter bird predation in beetles. Researchers collect the remains of the killed beetles (cases) and sample living beetles from the same area (controls), then compare how often the large-horn phenotype occurs in each group.

Why is OR needed?

The number of living and dead beetles in the dataset reflects sampling effort, not how often beetles actually get eaten.

Without the true number of beetles at risk, you can’t calculate the risk of being eaten, so there is no RR. The OR still works, because it comes out the same whether you sample by outcome (dead vs. alive) or by exposure (large vs. small horns).

Example 2

You can calculate the proportion of each group that experienced the event, and want to say how many times higher or lower it is → RR

Example: A two-year field experiment tracking 1,000 oak seedlings inside deer exclosures and 1,000 in adjacent open plots, with mortality as the target event.

All seedlings are counted at planting, so mortality risk is observed directly in each treatment. The RR gives the proportional change in mortality (e.g., “exclosures reduce mortality risk by 60%”) and is comparable across studies.

Because mortality is common in open plots (35%), the OR would be much smaller than the RR (0.30 vs. 0.40). If it were reported as a relative risk, it would overstate the effect of exclosures, reading as a 70% reduction instead of 60%.

Comparing odds ratio and risk ratio

Effect size What it compares Scale Main strength Main limitation
OR Odds between groups Relative Useful for case-control studies and logistic regression Less intuitive and can exaggerate relative effects for common events
RR Probability between groups Relative Direct and intuitive interpretation of relative risk Requires known risks and depends on baseline risk

The relationship between these two measures can be summarized simply:

  • OR vs. RR: Both describe relative effects, but the OR is based on odds and the RR is based on probability. They are approximately equivalent only when the outcome is rare.

Fig. 1 shows this directly. We hold the RR at 0.40 (as in the oak seedling example) and calculate the matching OR for each control-group risk.

Multiple events per individual

lnOR and lnRR are the most commonly used effect sizes for binary outcomes that can occur only a single time per individual (or plot or site). You can only die once.

Some events can happen to the same individual many times: infections, fires in a plot, breeding attempts. For these, the data are the number of events relative to how long individuals were monitored. These are called count or person-time data (a term borrowed from medicine; here the “persons” might be birds or vegetation plots).

There is no direct odds ratio for repeated events, because odds need a count of individuals that did and did not experience the event. You could record whether each individual had the event at least once, but this throws away information and depends on how long individuals were monitored. Count data should not be analyzed with lnOR, lnRR, or RD (higgins2024?).

The three main estimands are the log incidence rate ratio, incidence rate difference, and square-root transformed incidence rate difference.

  • Log incidence rate ratio (measure = "IRR"): the relative difference in event rates. This is the most commonly used (higgins2024?).
  • Incidence rate difference (measure = "IRD"): the absolute difference in event rates.
  • Square root transformed incidence rate difference (measure = "IRSD"): a transformed version of the IRD with more stable variance.

The same reasons we prefer ratio measures for 2×2 data apply here, so we recommend the IRR.

All three require the number of events in each group (x1i, x2i) and the total person-time in each group (t1i, t2i), which is the total time monitored across all individuals.

The data might look like this:

number_events number_individuals_monitored total_years_monitored treatment
5 15 5 experiment
15 15 5 control

First, calculate total person-time:

dat <- dat %>% 
  mutate(total_person_time = number_individuals_monitored * total_years_monitored)
knitr::kable(dat)

Then make the data wide:

dat_wide <- dat %>%

These effect sizes are less common in ecology and evolution, so we don’t cover them further here.

Time-to-event data

Sometimes what matters is not whether an event happened, but when. How long until a radio-collared deer dies? How many days until a nest is predated? How long until a seed germinates?

These are time-to-event data (also called survival data). Each individual contributes two pieces of information: how long it was followed, and whether the event happened by the end of that time.

The complication is censoring. Some individuals are followed without the event happening: the study ends, a collar fails, or an animal leaves the study area. We know they survived at least that long, but not how long they would have survived. These individuals still carry useful information, so they shouldn’t be thrown out.

What time-to-event data look like

A 2×2 table only records whether each individual had the event. Time-to-event data also record when. At the individual level, each row is one individual, with how long it was followed and whether the event happened:

Nest Group Days followed Event
1 Fenced 12 1 (predated)
2 Fenced 30 0 (survived to end of study)
3 Unfenced 5 1 (predated)
4 Unfenced 18 0 (nest lost track of)

Rows with a 0 are censored: we know the nest survived at least that long, but not what happened after.

Summed by group, the closest match to the 2×2 table at the top of this page is:

Events Censored Total Total time at risk
Exposed (e.g., Fenced) a b a + b \(T_1\)
Control (e.g., Unfenced) c d c + d \(T_2\)

The new column, total time at risk, is the days followed added up across all individuals in the group. Dividing events by time at risk gives an event rate for each group (\(a/T_1\) and \(c/T_2\)), and the ratio of the two rates estimates the hazard ratio (introduced below):

\[ HR \approx \frac{a / T_1}{c / T_2} \]

This is the same calculation as the incidence rate ratio in Multiple events per individual, so you can use escalc(measure = "IRR") with x1i = a, x2i = c, t1i = T1, and t2i = T2.

The catch is that this only gives the true HR if the hazard stays roughly constant over the study. If risk changes over time (e.g., nest predation peaks at hatching), the summary table loses that information, and an HR from a Cox model or log-rank test will be more accurate. Papers also rarely report total time at risk, so in practice you’ll usually extract a reported HR instead (see Getting hazard ratios from studies).

ImportantDon’t use mean time-to-event

It can be tempting to compare average survival time between groups, as you would for any continuous outcome (e.g., with lnRR or SMD). Don’t. You can only measure survival time for individuals that had the event during the study. Those still alive at the end don’t have a death date, so they get left out. Leaving them out skews the average, because the individuals left out are the ones that lived longest.

For example, if a study ends after 30 days, every animal that survived past day 30 is missing from the average. The group that survived best loses the most animals from the calculation, so the comparison can make it look worse than it is.

The hazard ratio

The standard effect size for time-to-event data is the hazard ratio (HR). The hazard is the risk of the event happening at a given moment, among individuals that haven’t experienced it yet. The HR compares this between groups:

  • HR = 1: The event happens at the same rate in both groups.
  • HR > 1: The event happens sooner (or more often at any given time) in the exposed group.
  • HR < 1: The event happens later (or less often at any given time) in the exposed group.

Most analyses assume the HR stays constant over the study period, even if the hazard itself rises and falls (e.g., nest predation peaks at hatching). This is called the proportional hazards assumption. If the effect clearly changes over time (e.g., a treatment protects seedlings in the first month but not after), a single HR can be misleading.

As with the OR and RR, HRs are log transformed for meta-analysis.

In plain English, an HR of 0.60 for nest predation in fenced vs. unfenced plots means:

At any point during the nesting period, nests in fenced plots were predated at 60% of the rate of nests in unfenced plots (a 40% lower hazard).

Note that this is not the same as “40% fewer nests were predated”. The HR describes the rate at which events happen, not the final proportion.

Getting hazard ratios from studies

escalc() can’t calculate HRs from summary data, because an HR needs the individual survival times. The ideal is to obtain the raw data from study authors and reanalyze it yourself, so every study is analyzed the same way. In practice, you will usually have to work from what the papers report, which often isn’t the statistic you need (higgins2024?).

There are three ways to get an HR from a published study. For practical, step-by-step guidance on all three, see (tierney2007?).

1. Extract the reported HR and its uncertainty. Studies that use a Cox proportional hazards model (or another survival regression) report an HR, usually with a 95% CI. You can convert these to lnHR and its standard error:

\[ \begin{aligned} &lnHR = \ln(HR) \\ &SE(lnHR) = \frac{\ln(CI_{upper}) - \ln(CI_{lower})}{3.92} \end{aligned} \]

# Hazard ratios for nest predation (fenced vs. unfenced) from two studies

2. Estimate it from a log-rank test. Many studies compare survival curves with a log-rank test rather than a Cox model. If you know the observed number of events in the exposed group (O), the number expected if the exposure had no effect (E), and the variance of the log-rank statistic (V), then (simmonds2011?):

\[ lnHR \approx \frac{O - E}{V}, \qquad SE(lnHR) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{V}} \]

This is the same calculation as the Peto odds ratio, applied to survival data. Studies rarely report O, E, and V directly, but they can often be worked out from the reported P value, the number of events in each group, and the survival curves (parmar1998?; williamson2002?). These methods involve several assumptions, so it’s worth involving a statistician.

The same calculation can be done on grouped data. For example, a nest study might report how many nests were predated each week and how many were still active at the end of each week. A log-rank analysis of these counts gives O, E, and V, but because the data are grouped, the HR is only approximate (this grouped estimate is sometimes called a log odds ratio in older literature, e.g., (ebctcg1990?)). Censored individuals need careful handling, and when the intervals are long relative to the study (e.g., yearly checks in a three-year study), methods for interval-censored survival are more appropriate (collett1994?).

3. Reconstruct it from a Kaplan–Meier curve. If a study only shows a survival curve, you can digitize the curve and reconstruct approximate individual data (guyot2012?). This lets you estimate the HR yourself, and also opens up other analyses of the survival data. It takes more work, so it’s best kept for studies that can’t be included any other way.

Converting to a 2×2 table instead

If every individual was followed for at least a fixed time (say 30 days), and studies report how many in each group had experienced the event by then, you can build a 2×2 table and use lnOR, lnRR, or RD as usual. This only works if no individuals were censored before that time point. It also throws away information about when events happened, so it’s best used when most studies report outcomes this way.

Don’t mix HRs with ORs or RRs in the same meta-analysis. They measure different things and only give similar values when events are rare.

#### Key points
- lnOR (log odds ratio) and lnRR (log risk ratios) describe discrete binary outcomes between two groups (i.e., experiment vs control)
- Odds ratios (OR) compare odds of a binary outcome occurring between two groups
- Risk ratios (RR) compare risks of the outcome. The two are close only when the event is rare.

# Log odds ratio (lnOR)

The **odds** of an event are the chance that it happens divided by the chance that it does not. For example, if 20 out of 100 individuals die, the odds of death are 20 to 80, or `r 20 / 80`. The **odds ratio (OR)** divides the odds of the event in the exposed group (group 1, the treatment) by the odds in the control group (group 2). An OR of 1 means the odds are the same in both groups.

The math is below. Here $a$ and $b$ are the number of individuals in the exposed group who did and did not have the event, and $c$ and $d$ are the same counts for the control group. In other words, $a$ is the exposed group with the event.

$$\begin{aligned}
&OR=\frac{a \times d}{b \times c} \\
\end{aligned}$${#eq-OR}

ORs are not analyzed directly in a meta-analysis because they are bounded at 0 and are not normally distributed. For example, an OR of 0.5 and an OR of 2 are equal and opposite effects, but they are not the same distance from 1. Log transforming fixes this: lnOR is centered on 0 and ranges from $-\infty$ to $\infty$.

$$\begin{aligned}
&lnOR = \ln(OR)\\
\end{aligned}$${#eq-lnOR}

## Interpreting lnOR

To interpret lnOR (including a pooled model estimate ± confidence intervals), exponentiate it ($e^{lnOR}$) to convert it back to an OR. Let's take our 

```{r}
or_a <- exp(dat_or$yi[1])
round(or_a, 1)
```

- **OR = 1:** Equal odds of the event in both groups (e.g., the stressor has no effect on the odds of infection).
- **OR > 1:** Higher odds of the event in the exposed group.
- **OR < 1:** Lower odds of the event in the exposed group.

There are several ways to describe an OR of `r round(or_a, 2)` in plain English, which are more or less comfortable based on whether OR is ≥ 1 or < 1.

1. If OR is ≥ 1, convert to percent change, using $100 \times (OR - 1)$:

> The odds of infection were `r round(100 * (or_a - 1))`% higher in the exposed group than in the control group.

2. You can also write in terms of multiples by rounding the odds ratio:

> The exposed group had `r round(or_a, 2)` times the odds of infection of the control group.

3. If the OR value is < 1, it might be easier to write from the control group's perspective, using $1/OR$:

> The control group had `r round(1 / or_a, 2)` times the odds of infection than the exposed group.

4. Similarly, when the OR is below 1, you can use $100 \times (1 - OR)$ to get the percentage decrease.

> The control group had `r round(100 * (1 - or_a), 2)`% reduction in the odds of infection.

Avoid describing an OR as "times more likely": that describes risk, not odds (see [Choosing an effect size](#sec-choosing)).

::: {.callout-note collapse="true"}
#### Click to understand why odds are not risk

Halving the odds is not the same as halving the risk. The change in risk depends on the baseline risk, $p_0$, which is the risk in the control group. Given an OR and a baseline risk, the risk in the exposed group is:

$$p_1 = \frac{OR \times p_0}{1 - p_0 + OR \times p_0}$$

The table below shows an OR of 0.5 at three different baseline risks:

:::

Variance for the OR is calculated on a natural logarithmic scale:

## Continuity correction

As you can see in @eq-lnOR and @eq-lnOR-var, if any cell ($a$, $b$, $c$, or $d$) is 0 (e.g., no individuals were infected in the control group), lnOR and its variance can't be calculated ($\frac{x}{0}=\infty$. By default, `escalc()` adds 0.5 to every cell of any table that contains a 0:


If there are no events (or only events) in *both* groups, the study gives no information about the OR and should be left out [@higgins2024]. You can do this by adding `drop00 = TRUE` in `escalc()` or you can manually exclude those data points.

# Log risk ratio (lnRR)

The Risk Ratio (RR), or relative risk, compares the probability (risk) of an event in the exposed group with the risk in the control group.Each risk is the number of individuals with the outcome divided by the total number of individuals in that group.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&n_1 = a + b\\
&n_2 = c + d\\
&RR = \frac{a/n_1}{c/n_2}
\end{aligned}
$${#eq-RR}

The RR is interpreted much like the OR, but it compares probabilities directly, which makes it easier to explain. It also has a ceiling. Because a risk cannot be larger than 1, $RR = p_1/p_0$ can never be larger than $1/p_0$, where $p_0$ is the control risk. For example, if half of the controls experience the event, the RR cannot be larger than `r 1 / 0.5`. This ceiling matters most when the event is common, and it is one reason lnRR can vary across studies with different baseline risks. The OR has no such ceiling.

As with the OR, the RR is log transformed for meta-analysis so that it is centered on 0 and approximately normal:

$$
lnRR = \ln(RR)
$${#eq-lnRR}

For Experiment 1, the lnRR is `r round(dat_rr$yi[1], 3)`, which is an RR of `r round(exp(dat_rr$yi[1]), 2)`.

Interpret RR as follows:

- **RR = 1:** Equal risk (or probability) between the comparison groups (e.g., a stressor has no effect on overall mortality risk).

- **RR > 1:** Higher risk (or probability) of the event in the exposed group.

- **RR < 1:** Lower risk (or probability) of the event in the exposed group.

In plain English, you can write the RR for Experiment 1 in two ways:

There are two ways to describe an RR of `r round(rr_a, 2)` in plain English:

1. Subtract 1 from the RR and multiply by 100 to get the relative change in risk: $100 \times (RR - 1)$. A positive value means higher risk in the exposed group, and a negative value means lower risk. When RR is below 1, $100 \times (1 - RR)$ is called the [Relative Risk Reduction](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-15#section-15-4).

> The outcome is `r round(100 * (exp(dat_rr$yi[1]) - 1))`% *more likely* in the exposed group than in the control group.

When the RR is below 1, use $100 \times (1 - RR)$ to get the percentage decrease. This is called the [relative risk reduction](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-15#section-15-4).

2. You can also report the RR directly, which is easier to interpret than the OR.

> The exposed group was `r round(exp(dat_rr$yi[1]), 2)` times as likely to experience the outcome as the control group.

:::callout-note
Notice that the OR for Wetland A (`r round(or_a, 2)`) is further from 1 than the RR (`r round(rr_a, 2)`). This gap grows as the event becomes more common.
:::

## Sampling variance

Like lnOR, lnRR variance is calculated on the log scale:

$$
Var(lnRR) = \frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{a + b} + \frac{1}{c} - \frac{1}{c + d}
$${#eq-lnRR-var}

## Continuity correction

lnRR breaks down when $a$ or $c$ equals 0, so it also needs a continuity correction. Like lnOR, `escalc` adds 0.5 to all four cells of the affected table, not only the affected group, so $n_1$ and $n_2$ each increase by 1. The same advice about studies with no events in either group applies here: set `drop00 = TRUE` unless you have a good reason to keep them.

:::callout-important
#### Defining the target event

You must clearly define your target event. It is easy to accidentally swap **success** with **failure**, or **fledged** with **failed**. Switching the target event (analyzing non-events instead of events) affects each effect size differently:

- **OR:** The new estimate becomes the exact reciprocal ($1/x$). In the 30% baseline example above, a mortality OR of `r or_fixed` becomes a survival OR of `r 1 / or_fixed`.

- **RR:** The estimate changes and is not simply the reciprocal. Its precision and significance can also change, sometimes substantially, which is a [troubling](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-10#section-10-4-3) mathematical property of RR. In the same 30% example, the mortality RR is `r round(rr_table[2], 2)`, but the survival RR is `r round((1 - p1_table[2]) / (1 - p0_table[2]), 2)`.

*Best practice:* Define the less common outcome as the target event. This reduces errors and heterogeneity between studies [@higgins2024]. Give your columns informative names to reduce data entry errors and utilize reproducible, trackable extraction practices, such as provided by the `R` package `metaDigitise`. We also recommend color-coding your columns in your spreadsheet to reduce confusion.
:::

# When to use lnOR or lnRR {#sec-choosing}

Start with your study design. If studies sampled by outcome (like the beetle example below), only the OR can be calculated, so the choice is made for you. If you can calculate risks, all three effect sizes are possible, and the choice comes down to a trade-off between statistical properties and interpretation.

**The case for lnOR**

- **It has no ceiling.** The RR is capped by the control group risk: if 50% of controls die, the exposed group can be at most twice as likely to die (RR ≤ 2). This means the same RR can be possible in one study and impossible in another with a higher baseline risk. The OR has no such cap.
- **It is symmetric.** If you swap the target event (e.g., survival instead of mortality), lnOR simply changes sign. lnRR changes in value, precision, and sometimes significance (see *Defining the target event* above).
- **It matches logistic regression.** Many studies report logistic regression coefficients, which are lnORs, so these can be combined directly with 2×2 data.

**The case for lnRR**

- **It is easier to understand.** "1.5 times as likely" is clearer to most readers than "1.5 times the odds".
- **It is not exaggerated when events are common.** When events are common (above about 10%), the OR is further from 1 than the RR, and readers often misread an OR as an RR, overstating the effect.

**When events are rare** (below about 10%), the OR and RR are nearly identical, and the choice between them matters little. However, continuity corrections can bias your results. See [below](#sec-rare-events) for discussion of other approaches.

**Our recommendation:** Run the meta-analysis on lnOR and lnRR to ensure that your results don't depend on this choice. If your estimates are similar, then convert the lnRR results to plain english for easier interpretability.

:::callout-note
#### Rare events{#sec-rare-events}

When events are very rare, the standard approach (calculating effect sizes with `escalc()` and pooling them with `rma.v()`) can give biased results. The continuity corrections and variance formulas it relies on were designed for larger counts. Based on simulation studies summarized in the [Cochrane Handbook](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-10#section-10-4-3):

- **Event risks below 1%:** If the group sizes are similar within studies and effects are not very large, use Peto's odds ratio. This method can be applied either by calculating odds ratio effect sizes with the PETO method (`escalc(measure = "PETO")`) and analyzing with `rma(yi, vi, method = "EE")` or inputting your cell counts (`ai`, `bi`, `ci`, `di`) directly into the model with `rma.peto()`, which will produce identical results. 

- **Event risks above 1%, very large effects, or unequal group sizes:** Use the Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio (`rma.mh()`, which needs no continuity correction) or a logistic regression model (`rma.glmm()`). As with `rma.peto()`, you submit your cell counts directly to `rma.glmm()`. This method directly models the outcomes without applying continuity corrections:

```{r}
#| eval: false
#| echo: true
res.glmm <- rma.glmm(ai=ai, bi=bi, ci=ci, di=di, 
                     measure="OR", 
                     data=dat)
```

Peto and Mantel–Haenszel are common-effect methods, so they don't estimate heterogeneity and don't allow you to control for non-independence structures in your data. Cochrane's view is that with sparse data, finding out whether there is any effect at all matters more than modeling heterogeneity. 

However, in ecology, where heterogeneity is king, we recommend using `rma.glmm()` in most cases, which can handle random effects. 

If you are dealing with very rare events (<1%), we recommend doing both `rma.peto()` and `rma.glmm()` and reporting the results of both approaches.
:::


### Applied ecological examples: matching the effect size to study design

The discussion so far has concerned the interpretatibility of lnOR and lnRR but the primary study designs you encounter add further limitations. We'll give a couple examples to help think this through.

**Example 1**

You cannot calculate the proportion of each group that experienced the event → OR only

Example: Testing whether horns deter bird predation in beetles. Researchers collect the remains of the killed beetles (cases) and sample living beetles from the same area (controls), then compare how often the large-horn phenotype occurs in each group.

Why is OR needed?

The number of living and dead beetles in the dataset reflects sampling effort, not how often beetles actually get eaten.

Without the true number of beetles at risk, you can't calculate the risk of being eaten, so there is no RR. The OR still works, because it comes out the same whether you sample by outcome (dead vs. alive) or by exposure (large vs. small horns).

**Example 2**

You can calculate the proportion of each group that experienced the event, and want to say how many times higher or lower it is → RR

Example: A two-year field experiment tracking 1,000 oak seedlings inside deer exclosures and 1,000 in adjacent open plots, with mortality as the target event.

```{r}
#| echo: false

# Oak seedling example: 35% mortality in open plots, and an RR of 0.40 inside exclosures
p0_oak <- 0.35
rr_oak <- 0.40
p1_oak <- rr_oak * p0_oak
or_oak <- (p1_oak / (1 - p1_oak)) / (p0_oak / (1 - p0_oak))
```

All seedlings are counted at planting, so mortality risk is observed directly in each treatment. The RR gives the proportional change in mortality (e.g., "exclosures reduce mortality risk by `r round(100 * (1 - rr_oak))`%") and is comparable across studies. 

Because mortality is common in open plots (`r round(100 * p0_oak)`%), the OR would be much smaller than the RR (`r sprintf("%.2f", or_oak)` vs. `r sprintf("%.2f", rr_oak)`). If it were reported as a relative risk, it would overstate the effect of exclosures, reading as a `r round(100 * (1 - or_oak))`% reduction instead of `r round(100 * (1 - rr_oak))`%.

### Comparing odds ratio and risk ratio

| Effect size | What it compares | Scale | Main strength | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **OR** | Odds between groups | Relative | Useful for case-control studies and logistic regression | Less intuitive and can exaggerate relative effects for common events |
| **RR** | Probability between groups | Relative | Direct and intuitive interpretation of relative risk | Requires known risks and depends on baseline risk |

The relationship between these two measures can be summarized simply:

- **OR vs. RR:** Both describe relative effects, but the OR is based on odds and the RR is based on probability. They are approximately equivalent only when the outcome is rare.

# Multiple events per individual {#sec-other-binary-outcomes}

lnOR and lnRR are the most commonly used effect sizes for binary outcomes that can occur only a single time per individual (or plot or site). You can only die once.

Some events can happen to the same individual many times: infections, fires in a plot, breeding attempts. For these, the data are the number of events relative to how long individuals were monitored. These are called count or person-time data (a term borrowed from medicine; here the "persons" might be birds or vegetation plots).

There is no direct odds ratio for repeated events, because odds need a count of individuals that did and did not experience the event. You could record whether each individual had the event at least once, but this throws away information and depends on how long individuals were monitored. Count data should not be analyzed with lnOR, lnRR, or RD [@higgins2024].

The three main **estimands** are the **log incidence rate ratio**, **incidence rate difference**, and **square-root transformed incidence rate difference**.

- **Log incidence rate ratio** (`measure = "IRR"`): the relative difference in event rates. This is the most commonly used [@higgins2024].
- **Incidence rate difference** (`measure = "IRD"`): the absolute difference in event rates.
- **Square root transformed incidence rate difference** (`measure = "IRSD"`): a transformed version of the IRD with more stable variance.

The same reasons we prefer ratio measures for 2×2 data apply here, so we recommend the IRR.

All three require the number of events in each group (`x1i`, `x2i`) and the total person-time in each group (`t1i`, `t2i`), which is the total time monitored across all individuals. 

The data might look like this:

```{r}
#| echo: false
dat <- data.frame(number_events = c(5, 15),
                  number_individuals_monitored = c(15, 15),
                  total_years_monitored = c(5, 5),
                  treatment = c("experiment", "control"))
knitr::kable(dat)
```

First, calculate total person-time:

```{r}
dat <- dat %>% 
  mutate(total_person_time = number_individuals_monitored * total_years_monitored)
knitr::kable(dat)
```

Make sure time units are the same across studies (e.g., convert months to years) before calculating.

Then make the data wide:

```{r}
dat_wide <- dat %>%
  select(treatment, number_events, total_person_time) %>%
  pivot_wider(names_from = "treatment",
              values_from = c("number_events", "total_person_time"))
  
knitr::kable(dat_wide)
```

Then calculate the log incidence rate ratio with `escalc()`:

```{r}
out <- escalc(measure = "IRR",
              data = dat_wide,
              x1i = number_events_experiment, x2i = number_events_control,
              t1i = total_person_time_experiment, t2i = total_person_time_control)

knitr::kable(out)
```

These effect sizes are less common in ecology and evolution, so we don't cover them further here.

# Time-to-event data {#sec-time-to-event}

Sometimes what matters is not *whether* an event happened, but *when*. How long until a radio-collared deer dies? How many days until a nest is predated? How long until a seed germinates?

These are time-to-event data (also called survival data). Each individual contributes two pieces of information: how long it was followed, and whether the event happened by the end of that time.

The complication is **censoring**. Some individuals are followed without the event happening: the study ends, a collar fails, or an animal leaves the study area. We know they survived *at least* that long, but not how long they would have survived. These individuals still carry useful information, so they shouldn't be thrown out.

## What time-to-event data look like

A 2×2 table only records *whether* each individual had the event. Time-to-event data also record *when*. At the individual level, each row is one individual, with how long it was followed and whether the event happened:

| **Nest** | **Group** | **Days followed** | **Event** |
| -------- | --------- | ----------------- | --------- |
| 1 | Fenced | 12 | 1 (predated) |
| 2 | Fenced | 30 | 0 (survived to end of study) |
| 3 | Unfenced | 5 | 1 (predated) |
| 4 | Unfenced | 18 | 0 (nest lost track of) |

Rows with a 0 are censored: we know the nest survived at least that long, but not what happened after.

Summed by group, the closest match to the 2×2 table at the top of this page is:

|                                          | **Events** | **Censored** | **Total** | **Total time at risk** |
| ---------------------------------------- | ---------- | ------------ | --------- | ---------------------- |
| **Exposed (e.g., Fenced)**               | a          | b            | a + b     | $T_1$                  |
| **Control (e.g., Unfenced)**             | c          | d            | c + d     | $T_2$                  |

The new column, total time at risk, is the days followed added up across all individuals in the group. Dividing events by time at risk gives an event rate for each group ($a/T_1$ and $c/T_2$), and the ratio of the two rates estimates the hazard ratio (introduced below):

$$
HR \approx \frac{a / T_1}{c / T_2}
$$

This is the same calculation as the incidence rate ratio in [Multiple events per individual](#sec-other-binary-outcomes), so you can use `escalc(measure = "IRR")` with `x1i = a`, `x2i = c`, `t1i = T1`, and `t2i = T2`.

The catch is that this only gives the true HR if the hazard stays roughly constant over the study. If risk changes over time (e.g., nest predation peaks at hatching), the summary table loses that information, and an HR from a Cox model or log-rank test will be more accurate. Papers also rarely report total time at risk, so in practice you'll usually extract a reported HR instead (see [Getting hazard ratios from studies](#sec-getting-hr)).

:::callout-important
#### Don't use mean time-to-event

It can be tempting to compare average survival time between groups, as you would for any continuous outcome (e.g., with lnRR or SMD). Don't. You can only measure survival time for individuals that had the event during the study. Those still alive at the end don't have a death date, so they get left out. Leaving them out skews the average, because the individuals left out are the ones that lived longest.

For example, if a study ends after 30 days, every animal that survived past day 30 is missing from the average. The group that survived best loses the most animals from the calculation, so the comparison can make it look worse than it is.
:::

## The hazard ratio

The standard effect size for time-to-event data is the **hazard ratio (HR)**. The hazard is the risk of the event happening at a given moment, among individuals that haven't experienced it yet. The HR compares this between groups:

- **HR = 1:** The event happens at the same rate in both groups.
- **HR > 1:** The event happens sooner (or more often at any given time) in the exposed group.
- **HR < 1:** The event happens later (or less often at any given time) in the exposed group.

Most analyses assume the HR stays constant over the study period, even if the hazard itself rises and falls (e.g., nest predation peaks at hatching). This is called the **proportional hazards assumption**. If the effect clearly changes over time (e.g., a treatment protects seedlings in the first month but not after), a single HR can be misleading.

As with the OR and RR, HRs are log transformed for meta-analysis.

In plain English, an HR of 0.60 for nest predation in fenced vs. unfenced plots means:

> At any point during the nesting period, nests in fenced plots were predated at 60% of the rate of nests in unfenced plots (a 40% lower hazard).

Note that this is *not* the same as "40% fewer nests were predated". The HR describes the rate at which events happen, not the final proportion.

## Getting hazard ratios from studies {#sec-getting-hr}

`escalc()` can't calculate HRs from summary data, because an HR needs the individual survival times. The ideal is to obtain the raw data from study authors and reanalyze it yourself, so every study is analyzed the same way. In practice, you will usually have to work from what the papers report, which often isn't the statistic you need [@higgins2024].

There are three ways to get an HR from a published study. For practical, step-by-step guidance on all three, see @tierney2007.

**1. Extract the reported HR and its uncertainty.** Studies that use a Cox proportional hazards model (or another survival regression) report an HR, usually with a 95% CI. You can convert these to lnHR and its standard error:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&lnHR = \ln(HR) \\
&SE(lnHR) = \frac{\ln(CI_{upper}) - \ln(CI_{lower})}{3.92}
\end{aligned}
$$

```{r}
# Hazard ratios for nest predation (fenced vs. unfenced) from two studies
dat_hr <- data.frame(
  study = c("Study A", "Study B"),
  hr = c(0.62, 0.80),
  ci_lb = c(0.41, 0.55),
  ci_ub = c(0.94, 1.16)
)

dat_hr <- dat_hr %>%
  mutate(yi = log(hr),
         sei = (log(ci_ub) - log(ci_lb)) / (2 * qnorm(0.975)),
         vi = sei^2)

knitr::kable(dat_hr, digits = 3)

# Meta-analysis on the log scale
res_hr <- rma(yi, vi, data = dat_hr)
res_hr

# Back-transform to an HR
predict(res_hr, transf = exp)
```

If a study reports an HR with an exact P value but no CI, you can recover the standard error from the P value [@higgins2024]:

$$
SE(lnHR) = \frac{|lnHR|}{z}, \qquad z = \Phi^{-1}(1 - P/2)
$$

```{r}
# e.g., HR = 0.70, P = 0.03
abs(log(0.70)) / qnorm(1 - 0.03 / 2)
```

This only works with exact P values. "P < 0.05" or "NS" doesn't give you enough information.

**2. Estimate it from a log-rank test.** Many studies compare survival curves with a log-rank test rather than a Cox model. If you know the observed number of events in the exposed group (O), the number expected if the exposure had no effect (E), and the variance of the log-rank statistic (V), then [@simmonds2011]:

$$
lnHR \approx \frac{O - E}{V}, \qquad SE(lnHR) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}
$$

This is the same calculation as the Peto odds ratio, applied to survival data. Studies rarely report O, E, and V directly, but they can often be worked out from the reported P value, the number of events in each group, and the survival curves [@parmar1998; @williamson2002]. These methods involve several assumptions, so it's worth involving a statistician.

The same calculation can be done on grouped data. For example, a nest study might report how many nests were predated each week and how many were still active at the end of each week. A log-rank analysis of these counts gives O, E, and V, but because the data are grouped, the HR is only approximate (this grouped estimate is sometimes called a log odds ratio in older literature, e.g., @ebctcg1990). Censored individuals need careful handling, and when the intervals are long relative to the study (e.g., yearly checks in a three-year study), methods for interval-censored survival are more appropriate [@collett1994].

**3. Reconstruct it from a Kaplan–Meier curve.** If a study only shows a survival curve, you can digitize the curve and reconstruct approximate individual data [@guyot2012]. This lets you estimate the HR yourself, and also opens up other analyses of the survival data. It takes more work, so it's best kept for studies that can't be included any other way.

## Converting to a 2×2 table instead

If every individual was followed for at least a fixed time (say 30 days), and studies report how many in each group had experienced the event by then, you can build a 2×2 table and use lnOR, lnRR, or RD as usual. This only works if no individuals were censored before that time point. It also throws away information about *when* events happened, so it's best used when most studies report outcomes this way.

Don't mix HRs with ORs or RRs in the same meta-analysis. They measure different things and only give similar values when events are rare.
