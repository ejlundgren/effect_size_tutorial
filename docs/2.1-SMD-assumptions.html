Comparing continuous outcomes between two groups

Standardized mean differences, log ratios of means, and related biological questions

Published

September 1, 2026

What biological question are you asking?

Many biological studies compare a continuous response between two groups. For example:

  • Do animals exposed to a treatment weigh more than control animals?

  • Does warming change plant biomass?

  • Are prey less abundant where predators are present?

  • Does a treatment change the magnitude of a behavioural response, regardless of whether that response is positive or negative?

Studies like these often report the same basic information: a mean, standard deviation, and sample size for each group. However, the same data can answer several different biological questions.

Before choosing an effect size, ask what kind of difference matters:

Biological question Effect size
How far apart are the group means relative to variation among observations? Standardized mean difference (SMD)
How many times larger or smaller is one group mean than the other? Log-transformed ratio of means (lnRoM)
Does one group have responses of greater magnitude, regardless of direction? lnM
Is the response more variable in one group? lnVR or lnCVR

These effect sizes may use similar information, but they do not answer the same question. The biological question—not simply the format in which the data were reported—should determine which one you use.

In this chapter, SMD and lnRoM are equally important options for comparing continuous responses between two groups. Neither is universally better. The appropriate choice depends on the biological quantity you want to estimate and whether the data meet the assumptions of that effect size.

ImportantlnRoM and lnRR are different effect sizes

The log-transformed ratio of means (lnRoM) compares the means of continuous responses such as weight, biomass, abundance, growth, or concentration. It is often called the log response ratio.

The log risk ratio (lnRR) compares the risks of binary events such as survival, infection, or fledging. We discuss lnRR in the chapter on binary outcomes.

The names are similar, but these effect sizes describe different kinds of data and answer different questions. Throughout this tutorial, we use lnRoM for the log ratio of means and reserve lnRR for the log risk ratio.

A running biological example

Imagine a warming experiment that measures plant biomass after one growing season:

Group Mean biomass Standard deviation Sample size
Ambient control 10 g 2 g 20 plants
Warming treatment 12 g 3 g 20 plants

The treatment mean is 2 g higher than the control mean. SMD will express that difference relative to variation among plants, whereas lnRoM will express it as a proportional change. We will use this example to develop and interpret both measures side by side.

Before you calculate an effect size

Decide the direction of the comparison

Before extracting data, decide which group will be group 1 and which will be group 2. Keep this choice consistent across every study. In this chapter, we use:

  • group 1: treatment;

  • group 2: control.

For SMD, the numerator is therefore:

\[ m_1-m_2 = m_{treatment}-m_{control}. \]

For lnRoM, the ratio is:

\[ \frac{m_1}{m_2} = \frac{m_{treatment}}{m_{control}}. \]

With this convention, a positive SMD or lnRoM means that the treatment response is higher than the control response; a negative value means that it is lower.

Record the required information

For the standard versions of SMD and lnRoM, extract the following information for both groups:

  • the mean: \(m_1\) and \(m_2\);

  • the standard deviation: \(sd_1\) and \(sd_2\);

  • the sample size: \(n_1\) and \(n_2\).

The means determine the observed difference or ratio. The standard deviations and sample sizes help quantify uncertainty. For SMD, the standard deviations are also part of the effect-size point estimate itself. We will introduce each role step by step below.

WarningMeasurements of error/variance

The effect sizes in this section require standard deviation (\(sd\)) for the treatment and control groups. \(sd\) is fundamentally distinct from standard error (\(se\)). Some meta-analyses have mistaken the two, with egregious results. Be sure to convert all measurements of error to \(sd\). Other commonly reported forms of error include 95% confidence intervals (or 90%), variance, and interquartile ranges (e.g., in boxplots). The extraction package metaDigitise() automatically converts \(se\), interquartile ranges, and 95% CIs to \(sd\).

For a group with sample size \(n\), standard error (\(SE\)) and standard deviation (\(s\)) are related by:

\[ s = SE\sqrt{n}. \tag{1}\]

Standard deviation is the square root of the variance:

\[ s = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}}. \tag{2}\]

If a symmetric confidence interval describes a group mean, first recover its standard error and then convert that standard error to a standard deviation:

\[ \begin{aligned} SE &= \frac{CI_{upper}-CI_{lower}}{2c},\\ s &= SE\sqrt{n}, \end{aligned} \tag{3}\]

where \(c\) is the critical value used to construct the interval. For a large-sample 95% confidence interval based on the normal distribution, \(c=1.96\). For small samples or intervals based on another distribution, use the critical value reported or implied by the study rather than automatically using 1.96.

How do SMD and lnRoM work?

SMD and lnRoM begin with the same two group means, but they scale the comparison differently. We introduce each calculation one step at a time.

Standardized mean difference

Step 1: calculate the raw difference in means

With group 1 defined as treatment and group 2 as control, the raw mean difference is:

\[ MD = m_1-m_2. \tag{4}\]

For the plant-biomass example, \(MD=12-10=2\) g. This result is easy to interpret, but grams cannot be compared directly with studies that measured biomass in kilograms or used another response scale.

Step 2: calculate the pooled standard deviation

SMD places the mean difference on a common scale by dividing it by an estimate of the standard deviation shared by the two groups. For the conventional equal-variance estimator, the pooled standard deviation is:

\[ s_{pooled}= \sqrt{ \frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2+(n_2-1)s_2^2} {n_1+n_2-2} }. \tag{5}\]

Here, \(s_1\) and \(s_2\) are the reported group standard deviations. Each group variance is weighted by its degrees of freedom, \(n-1\).

For the plant-biomass example:

\[ s_{pooled}= \sqrt{\frac{19(3^2)+19(2^2)}{38}} =\sqrt{6.5} \approx 2.55\ \mathrm{g}. \]

Step 3: calculate Cohen’s d

The most widely recognized form of the standardized mean difference is Cohen’s \(d\). It is the natural next step in the calculation: we take the raw difference from Step 1 and divide it by the pooled standard deviation from Step 2.

Cohen’s \(d\) is commonly used to explain and report standardized differences in individual studies. In meta-analysis, however, we usually apply one additional small-sample correction to obtain Hedges’ \(g\), which we introduce in Step 4.

Cohen’s \(d\) is calculated as:

\[ d=\frac{m_1-m_2}{s_{pooled}}. \tag{6}\]

For the plant-biomass example:

\[ d=\frac{12-10}{2.55}\approx 0.78. \]

The treatment and control means are therefore separated by about 0.78 pooled standard deviations. The standard deviation here represents observed variation among measurements; it should not automatically be interpreted as measurement error or meaningless “noise.”

Step 4: correct small-sample bias

Cohen’s \(d\) tends to overestimate the population standardized mean difference when samples are small. Hedges’ \(g\) applies a correction factor, \(J\), to reduce this bias (Hedges 1981):

\[ \begin{aligned} df &= n_1+n_2-2,\\ J &\approx 1-\frac{3}{4df-1},\\ g &= Jd. \end{aligned} \tag{7}\]

The displayed expression for \(J\) is a widely used approximation to the exact correction. As sample size increases, \(J\) approaches 1, so \(g\) approaches \(d\). For the plant-biomass example, \(df=38\), \(J\approx0.980\), and \(g\approx0.77\).

In this tutorial, SMD generally refers to bias-corrected Hedges’ \(g\). The metafor::escalc() function applies this correction by default when measure = "SMD"; setting correct = FALSE returns the uncorrected Cohen’s \(d\).

Log-transformed ratio of means

SMD scales the difference in means by observed variation. lnRoM instead compares one positive mean directly with the other positive mean.

Step 1: calculate the ratio of means

For the plant-biomass example:

\[ \frac{m_1}{m_2}=\frac{12}{10}=1.2. \]

The treatment mean is therefore 1.2 times the control mean, or 20% higher.

Step 2: take the natural logarithm

The uncorrected log ratio of means is:

\[ L=\ln\left(\frac{m_1}{m_2}\right). \tag{8}\]

For the plant-biomass example:

\[ L=\ln(1.2)\approx0.182. \]

The logarithm makes equal proportional increases and decreases comparable around zero. A ratio of 1 becomes \(\ln(1)=0\); reciprocal ratios have equal magnitudes and opposite signs.

lnRoM is intended for measurements on a ratio scale with a meaningful zero, and the group means should be positive. It is not suitable when the means have opposite signs or when zero is arbitrary, as with temperature measured in degrees Celsius.

Step 3: consider the small-sample correction

The simple log ratio in Equation 8 can be biased, especially with small samples and large coefficients of variation. A second-order correction is (Lajeunesse 2015):

\[ L^{\ast}=\ln\left(\frac{m_1}{m_2}\right) +\frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{s_1^2}{n_1m_1^2} -\frac{s_2^2}{n_2m_2^2} \right). \tag{9}\]

The added term becomes smaller as sample sizes increase. Unlike the pooled standard deviation in SMD, this term is a bias correction; it does not redefine lnRoM as a standardized mean difference.

Current versions of metafor::escalc() apply the lnRoM bias correction by default when measure = "ROM". Use correct = FALSE to request the uncorrected value:

Calculate and interpret both effect sizes in R

We can now calculate both effect sizes for the plant-biomass example. Group 1 is the warming treatment and group 2 is the ambient control, following the direction chosen above.

First calculate bias-corrected SMD (Hedges’ \(g\)):

escalc(m1i = 12, sd1i = 3, n1i = 20,
       m2i = 10, sd2i = 2, n2i = 20,
       measure = "SMD")

      yi     vi 
1 0.7689 0.1074

The expected SMD is approximately 0.77. In plain language, mean biomass in the warming treatment is about 0.77 pooled standard deviations higher than in the ambient control.

Values such as 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 are sometimes labelled “small,” “medium,” and “large.” These generic labels are not universal biological thresholds. Whether an SMD is biologically important depends on the response, organisms, intervention, study design, and consequences of the change. Whenever possible, interpret the estimate using domain knowledge and report its uncertainty.

Now calculate the uncorrected lnRoM from the same summary statistics:

escalc(m1i = 12, sd1i = 3, n1i = 20,
       m2i = 10, sd2i = 2, n2i = 20,
       measure = "ROM",
       correct = FALSE)

      yi     vi 
1 0.1823 0.0051

The uncorrected lnRoM is \(\ln(12/10)\approx0.182\). It can be converted to proportional or percentage change, with \(y_i\) denoting lnRoM:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{proportional change} &= e^{y_i}-1,\\ \text{percentage change} &= 100(e^{y_i}-1). \end{aligned} \tag{10}\]

In R:

(exp(log(12 / 10)) - 1) * 100
[1] 20

The result is a 20% increase in mean biomass under warming. This percentage interpretation is useful only when a proportional comparison is biologically meaningful and the response is measured on a ratio scale with a meaningful zero.

The two estimates answer the questions introduced at the start of the chapter:

  • SMD: biomass is about 0.77 pooled standard deviations higher under warming.

  • lnRoM: mean biomass is 20% higher under warming.

Neither statement is a better summary of the experiment in every context. The appropriate interpretation depends on whether the biological question concerns a difference relative to variation or a proportional difference.

The following properties help determine whether each interpretation is mathematically appropriate:

Data property SMD lnRoM
Are group standard deviations part of the point estimate? Yes No, except in the small-sample bias correction
Must the response have a meaningful zero? No Yes
Must both group means be positive? No Yes
Can the estimate be back-transformed directly to a percentage? No Yes

The remaining sections examine other considerations—including bounded responses, dimensionality, non-normality, overdispersion, heteroscedasticity, and missing standard deviations—without assuming that either effect size is generally more robust.

Add a separate analysis of variability

A treatment can change both the mean and the variability of a biological response (Nakagawa et al. 2015). An lnRoM analysis describes the proportional change in the group means, but it does not tell us whether observations became more or less variable within the groups.

When variability is biologically relevant, we recommend adding a separate analysis using the log variability ratio (lnVR):

\[ \mathrm{lnVR}=\ln\left(\frac{s_1}{s_2}\right), \tag{11}\]

where \(s_1\) is the treatment-group standard deviation and \(s_2\) is the control-group standard deviation. With the direction used throughout this chapter:

  • \(\mathrm{lnVR}>0\): the treatment group is more variable;

  • \(\mathrm{lnVR}=0\): the groups have equal standard deviations;

  • \(\mathrm{lnVR}<0\): the treatment group is less variable.

For the plant-biomass example:

\[ \mathrm{lnVR}=\ln\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)\approx0.405. \]

Back-transforming gives \(e^{0.405}\approx1.5\), so the standard deviation is approximately 50% higher under warming. The lnRoM and lnVR analyses together provide a more complete biological description: warming increased mean biomass by 20% and was also associated with greater variation among plants.

Calculate lnVR as a separate outcome rather than combining lnRoM and lnVR values in one univariate model:

escalc(measure = "VR",
       sd1i = 3, n1i = 20,
       sd2i = 2, n2i = 20)

      yi     vi 
1 0.4055 0.0526

Interpret the two analyses separately. They address related but distinct questions: lnRoM concerns proportional differences in means, whereas lnVR concerns ratios of standard deviations.

WarningUse lnCVR carefully: its value depends directly on the means

The log coefficient of variation ratio (lnCVR) compares standard deviations after dividing each one by its group mean (Nakagawa et al. 2015):

\[ \mathrm{lnCVR} =\ln\left(\frac{s_1/m_1}{s_2/m_2}\right) =\mathrm{lnVR}-\mathrm{lnRoM}, \tag{12}\]

before any small-sample bias corrections. Because the means occur directly in this formula, a change in lnCVR can arise from a change in the standard deviations, a change in the means, or both.

lnCVR is intended to account for a common biological pattern: groups with larger means often also have larger variances. However, dividing by the mean makes an implicit scaling assumption. In simple terms, it works most naturally when the standard deviation increases roughly in proportion to the mean. Equivalently, variance then increases roughly with the square of the mean.

That relationship differs among data types:

  • Approximately normal data with constant variance: the standard deviation may stay similar as the mean changes. The coefficient of variation will then change mechanically with the mean.

  • Poisson count data: variance equals the mean, so standard deviation increases with the square root of the mean rather than directly with the mean. The coefficient of variation therefore decreases as the mean increases.

  • Negative-binomial count data: variance can increase much faster than the mean. At large means, the standard deviation may be close to proportional to the mean, but the relationship depends on the dispersion parameter.

  • Proportions and other bounded responses: variance is constrained by the mean and commonly shrinks near the boundaries. The mean–variance relationship can therefore be strong and nonlinear.

For these reasons, do not choose lnCVR automatically. First plot or model the relationship between group means and standard deviations across the dataset. Use lnCVR when relative variability is the intended estimand and its assumed mean–variance scaling is plausible. Otherwise, report lnVR alongside lnRoM, examine the mean–variance relationship directly, or consider an arm-based model of means and standard deviations.

When are SMD and lnRoM appropriate?

Values ≤ 0

A key consideration in choosing between SMD and lnRoM is whether your dataset has means ≤ 0. These may occur for a variety of reasons, including because of before-after-control-impact experimental designs or other data presentation formats. If means ≤ 0 are common in your dataset then you may have to use SMD simply in order to make use of all of your data.

ImportantData bounded between 0 and 1

Means±SD bounded between 0 and 1 are challenged by their numeric properties: variance tends to retreat to zero when the mean approaches 0 or 1 (Macartney et al. 2022).

To resolve this for SMD and lnRoM effect sizes, you need to transform both the means and the standard deviations, using an arcsin-square root transformation on the mean and an equation derived using the delta method to standardize the standard deviation (see Macartney et al. (2022)).

Dimensionality

The effect sizes in this section are standardized, which allow us to compare results across studies even if their units were different. However, what if one study measured a response in meters but another study measured it in area (\(meter^2\)) and a third study measured the same response in volume (\(meter^3\))?

Imagine vegetation studies or morphological measurements of an organism or the size of a tumor. Depending on the effect size you choose, the results can be strongly affected by these added dimensions—which is an overlooked problem in meta-analysis.

For lnRoM, this is a very serious problem—the estimated effect at dimension 1 will double at dimension two!

For this reason, we suggest that if you are committed to using an lnRoM effect size, that you must record the number of dimensions of each measurement. You can use these to divide the resulting effect size point estimate by the number of dimensions to standardize dimensionality. E.g., if the measurement was across 2 dimensions (\(m^2\)) then divide by 2 and if across 3 dimensions (\(m^3\)), divide by 3.

However, there may be many cases where the number of dimensions of the measurement may be unclear. In this case, you must use SMD. We demonstrate this in the hidden code block below.

Important

The dimensionality problem of lnRoM is quite perilous given that lnRoM is the dominant effect size used in ecology and evolution. It may be that the majority of ecological meta-analyses need to be redone.

Overdispersion

SMD and lnRoM assume that the underlying data generation process is normal. This assumption can be broken if the data is overdispersed—which is remarkably common in ecology and evolution, especially with abundance or count data. This can lead to biased point and sampling variance estimates.

There are as yet no effect sizes formulated for overdispersed data. As such, if there is overdispersed data in your dataset, you should:

  • Exclude effect sizes that are overdispersed based on rules of thumb we’ll give below as a sensitivity analysis in comparison to your full dataset

  • Use the inverse of effective sample size as your meta-analytic weight, instead of sampling variance, as an additional sensitivity analysis.

As you’ll see throughout this tutorial, sensitivity tests are the annoying reality of meta-analysis.

Testing for overdispersion should be part of your analytic workflow. The easiest way to test for overdispersion is to calculate the coefficient of variation (\(CV\)) for your control and treatment groups. \(CV\) is the ratio of standard deviation to the mean (\(sd/x\)).

We consider a \(CV>2\) to be indicative of overdispersion. However, for lnRoM, Lajeunesse (2015) suggested a small sample-size corrected threshold based on the standardized mean, derived from the Geary test Hedges et al. (1999). Let \(m\) be the group mean, \(sd\) be the standard deviation and \(n\) be your group sample size:

\[ \begin{aligned} G' = \frac{m}{sd}*\frac{4*n^{3/2}}{1+4*n} \end{aligned} \tag{13}\]

There are several suggested thresholds for this value that can be used to exclude potentially overdispersed and problematic data. Lajuennesse suggested excluding values ≥3.

If a lot of your data is overdispersed, one option is to compare the bias-corrected and uncorrected lnRoM estimators as a sensitivity analysis. In current versions of metafor, request the uncorrected estimator with correct = FALSE; it is not the default. We also recommend conducting sensitivity analyses that exclude data identified as potentially problematic using clearly specified criteria, and comparing sampling-variance weights with effective-sample-size weights when justified (Nakagawa et al. 2023).

Click to see a demonstration of overdispersion

Let’s do this in R with a \(CV>2\) threshold and the Geary-Lajuennesse threshold of 3, using a dataset provided by the metafor package:

data("dat.gibson2002")
dat <- dat.gibson2002

# Drop NA values for the SMD type effect sizes
dat <- dat %>% filter(!is.na(m1i))

# Calculate coefficient of variation:
dat <- dat %>%
  mutate(cv1 = sd1i / m1i,
         cv2 = sd2i / m2i)

n_overdispersed <- dat %>% 
  filter(cv1 > 2 | cv2 > 2) %>%
  nrow()
n_overdispersed/nrow(dat) * 100
[1] 61.53846

It looks like 46% of this dataset has a \(CV>2\)!

# With the Geary-Lajuennesse threshold:
geary <- function(sd, m, n){
  (m/sd) * (4*n^(3/2))/(1 + 4*n)
}

dat <- dat %>%
  mutate(gl1 = geary(sd = sd1i, m = m1i, n = n1i),
         gl2 = geary(sd = sd2i, m = m2i, n = n2i))


n_overdispersed <- dat %>% 
  filter(gl1 > 3 | gl2 > 3) %>%
  nrow()
n_overdispersed/nrow(dat) * 100
[1] 76.92308

It looks like ~54% the data in this meta-analysis fails the Geary-Lajuennesse test. So what do you do if that happens? We suggest that you filter out overdispersed values and rerun your models, both with the full dataset and with the subsets that are not overdispersed.

An alternative approach is to use the inverse of effective sample size as your meta-analytic model weight, instead of sampling variance. This may be an essential method when a significant amount of your data is overdispersed. This is a method that produces reasonable meta-analytic estimates, despite ignoring standard deviation (Nakagawa et al. 2023).

Effective sample size is defined as: \[ \begin{aligned} N_{0} = \frac{4n_1n_2}{n_1 + n_2}\\ \end{aligned} \tag{14}\]

Heteroscedasticity

Heteroscedasticity (i.e., differences in variance between the experimental groups) can bias effect sizes considerably. SMD has a special formulation that accounts for heteroscedasticity (Bonett 2009). This is available as the measure ‘SMDH’ in metafor::escalc.

For the uncorrected lnRoM, the point estimate depends only on the two means, so unequal group variances do not change that point estimate. However, the group variances do affect the sampling variance and the small-sample bias correction. Heteroscedasticity therefore still matters for uncertainty and for the choice of estimator.

Conclusion—which effect size should you use?

Return first to the biological question. Use SMD when the difference between groups should be expressed relative to variation among observations. Use lnRoM when the proportional difference between positive means is the quantity of interest and the response has a meaningful zero. The flow chart below summarizes additional considerations.

Alternative estimators

Paired designs

There are alternative estimators for SMD and lnRoM can account for paired designs, that is for correlated variance between groups 1 and 2.

In some cases, effect sizes derived from paired designs can be compared directly to those from unpaired designs. However, this is not always clear. There are a myriad of estimators for SMD-style effect sizes that account for pre-post treatment designs (see our escalc cheatsheet). These options may preferable if your meta-analysis focuses only on these types of experiments.

However, these estimators are not comparable to normal, unpaired SMD estimators. Our eff_size function provides a paired option for SMD that is comparable to unpaired SMD estimates, which we demonstrate below. escalc (as well as eff_size) provides an paired lnRoM estimator that is comparable to the standard unpaired estimator, which we also demonstrate below.

In paired design estimators, the variance estimation formulas accept an additional argument \(\rho\)—the correlation between plots or individuals, which is pronounced “rho” and symbolized as r in most R code. Since \(\rho\) is usually unknown and unreported, it’s general practice to set it to 0.5 or 0.8 and to run sensitivity analyses to ensure that that choice isn’t unduly influencing your model estimates.

Note\(\rho\)

The correlation between groups, \(\rho\) (ri in escalc and r in eff_size) is rarely reported and so needs to be assigned based, basically, on guesswork. We’d suggest that if the pairing is within an individual or plot (e.g., before-after a treatment), then a \(\rho\) of 0.8 seems reasonable. However, if the pairing is based on spatially paired but independent plots (e.g., adjacent to each other across a fenceline) then a rho of 0.5 may be more reasonable.

Be sure to always conduct sensitivity analyses on these choices, to ensure that they aren’t unduly changing your model estimates.

# What biological question are you asking? {#sec-biological-question}

Many biological studies compare a continuous response between two groups. For example:

- Do animals exposed to a treatment weigh more than control animals?

- Does warming change plant biomass?

- Are prey less abundant where predators are present?

- Does a treatment change the magnitude of a behavioural response, regardless of whether that response is positive or negative?

Studies like these often report the same basic information: a mean, standard deviation, and sample size for each group. However, the same data can answer several different biological questions.

Before choosing an effect size, ask what kind of difference matters:

| Biological question | Effect size |
|---|---|
| How far apart are the group means relative to variation among observations? | Standardized mean difference (**SMD**) |
| How many times larger or smaller is one group mean than the other? | Log-transformed ratio of means (**lnRoM**) |
| Does one group have responses of greater magnitude, regardless of direction? | **lnM** |
| Is the response more variable in one group? | **lnVR** or **lnCVR** |

These effect sizes may use similar information, but they do not answer the same question. The biological question---not simply the format in which the data were reported---should determine which one you use.

In this chapter, SMD and lnRoM are equally important options for comparing continuous responses between two groups. Neither is universally better. The appropriate choice depends on the biological quantity you want to estimate and whether the data meet the assumptions of that effect size.



:::callout-important
### lnRoM and lnRR are different effect sizes

The **log-transformed ratio of means (lnRoM)** compares the means of continuous responses such as weight, biomass, abundance, growth, or concentration. It is often called the *log response ratio*.

The **log risk ratio (lnRR)** compares the risks of binary events such as survival, infection, or fledging. We discuss lnRR in the chapter on [binary outcomes](2.2-lnOR-assumptions.qmd).

The names are similar, but these effect sizes describe different kinds of data and answer different questions. Throughout this tutorial, we use **lnRoM** for the log ratio of means and reserve **lnRR** for the log risk ratio.
:::

# A running biological example {#sec-running-example}

Imagine a warming experiment that measures plant biomass after one growing season:

| Group | Mean biomass | Standard deviation | Sample size |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Ambient control | 10 g | 2 g | 20 plants |
| Warming treatment | 12 g | 3 g | 20 plants |

The treatment mean is 2 g higher than the control mean. SMD will express that difference relative to variation among plants, whereas lnRoM will express it as a proportional change. We will use this example to develop and interpret both measures side by side.

# Before you calculate an effect size {#sec-before-calculation}

## Decide the direction of the comparison {#sec-direction-convention}

Before extracting data, decide which group will be group 1 and which will be group 2. Keep this choice consistent across every study. In this chapter, we use:

- group 1: treatment;

- group 2: control.

For SMD, the numerator is therefore:

$$
m_1-m_2 = m_{treatment}-m_{control}.
$$

For lnRoM, the ratio is:

$$
\frac{m_1}{m_2} = \frac{m_{treatment}}{m_{control}}.
$$

With this convention, a positive SMD or lnRoM means that the treatment response is higher than the control response; a negative value means that it is lower.

## Record the required information {#sec-required-information}

For the standard versions of SMD and lnRoM, extract the following information for both groups:

- the mean: $m_1$ and $m_2$;

- the standard deviation: $sd_1$ and $sd_2$;

- the sample size: $n_1$ and $n_2$.

The means determine the observed difference or ratio. The standard deviations and sample sizes help quantify uncertainty. For SMD, the standard deviations are also part of the effect-size point estimate itself. We will introduce each role step by step below.

::: callout-warning
######### Measurements of error/variance {#sec-callout-error-types}

The effect sizes in this section require standard deviation ($sd$) for the treatment and control groups. $sd$ is fundamentally distinct from standard error ($se$). Some meta-analyses have mistaken the two, with egregious results. Be sure to convert all measurements of error to $sd$. Other commonly reported forms of error include 95% confidence intervals (or 90%), variance, and interquartile ranges (e.g., in boxplots). The extraction package `metaDigitise()` automatically converts $se$, interquartile ranges, and 95% CIs to $sd$.

For a group with sample size $n$, standard error ($SE$) and standard deviation ($s$) are related by:

$$
s = SE\sqrt{n}.
$${#eq-se-to-sd}

Standard deviation is the square root of the variance:

$$
s = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}}.
$${#eq-var-to-sd}

If a symmetric confidence interval describes a group mean, first recover its standard error and then convert that standard error to a standard deviation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
SE &= \frac{CI_{upper}-CI_{lower}}{2c},\\
s  &= SE\sqrt{n},
\end{aligned}
$${#eq-ci-to-sd}

where $c$ is the critical value used to construct the interval. For a large-sample 95% confidence interval based on the normal distribution, $c=1.96$. For small samples or intervals based on another distribution, use the critical value reported or implied by the study rather than automatically using 1.96.
:::



# How do SMD and lnRoM work? {#sec-how-effect-sizes-work}

SMD and lnRoM begin with the same two group means, but they scale the comparison differently. We introduce each calculation one step at a time.

## Standardized mean difference {#sec-smd-math}

### Step 1: calculate the raw difference in means

With group 1 defined as treatment and group 2 as control, the raw mean difference is:

$$
MD = m_1-m_2.
$${#eq-raw-mean-difference}

For the plant-biomass example, $MD=12-10=2$ g. This result is easy to interpret, but grams cannot be compared directly with studies that measured biomass in kilograms or used another response scale.

### Step 2: calculate the pooled standard deviation

SMD places the mean difference on a common scale by dividing it by an estimate of the standard deviation shared by the two groups. For the conventional equal-variance estimator, the pooled standard deviation is:

$$
s_{pooled}=
\sqrt{
\frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2+(n_2-1)s_2^2}
{n_1+n_2-2}
}.
$${#eq-pooled-sd}

Here, $s_1$ and $s_2$ are the reported group standard deviations. Each group variance is weighted by its degrees of freedom, $n-1$.

For the plant-biomass example:

$$
s_{pooled}=
\sqrt{\frac{19(3^2)+19(2^2)}{38}}
=\sqrt{6.5}
\approx 2.55\ \mathrm{g}.
$$

### Step 3: calculate Cohen's d

The most widely recognized form of the standardized mean difference is **Cohen's $d$**. It is the natural next step in the calculation: we take the raw difference from Step 1 and divide it by the pooled standard deviation from Step 2.

Cohen's $d$ is commonly used to explain and report standardized differences in individual studies. In meta-analysis, however, we usually apply one additional small-sample correction to obtain **Hedges' $g$**, which we introduce in Step 4.

Cohen's $d$ is calculated as:

$$
d=\frac{m_1-m_2}{s_{pooled}}.
$${#eq-cohens-d}

For the plant-biomass example:

$$
d=\frac{12-10}{2.55}\approx 0.78.
$$

The treatment and control means are therefore separated by about 0.78 pooled standard deviations. The standard deviation here represents observed variation among measurements; it should not automatically be interpreted as measurement error or meaningless “noise.”

### Step 4: correct small-sample bias

Cohen's $d$ tends to overestimate the population standardized mean difference when samples are small. Hedges' $g$ applies a correction factor, $J$, to reduce this bias [@Hedges1981-bt]:

$$
\begin{aligned}
df &= n_1+n_2-2,\\
J &\approx 1-\frac{3}{4df-1},\\
g &= Jd.
\end{aligned}
$${#eq-hedges-g}

The displayed expression for $J$ is a widely used approximation to the exact correction. As sample size increases, $J$ approaches **1**, so $g$ approaches $d$. For the plant-biomass example, $df=38$, $J\approx0.980$, and $g\approx0.77$.

In this tutorial, **SMD** generally refers to bias-corrected Hedges' $g$. The `metafor::escalc()` function applies this correction by default when `measure = "SMD"`; setting `correct = FALSE` returns the uncorrected Cohen's $d$.

## Log-transformed ratio of means {#sec-lnrom-math}

SMD scales the difference in means by observed variation. lnRoM instead compares one positive mean directly with the other positive mean.

### Step 1: calculate the ratio of means

For the plant-biomass example:

$$
\frac{m_1}{m_2}=\frac{12}{10}=1.2.
$$

The treatment mean is therefore 1.2 times the control mean, or 20% higher.

### Step 2: take the natural logarithm

The uncorrected log ratio of means is:

$$
L=\ln\left(\frac{m_1}{m_2}\right).
$${#eq-lnrom-first-order}

For the plant-biomass example:

$$
L=\ln(1.2)\approx0.182.
$$

The logarithm makes equal proportional increases and decreases comparable around zero. A ratio of 1 becomes $\ln(1)=0$; reciprocal ratios have equal magnitudes and opposite signs.

lnRoM is intended for measurements on a ratio scale with a meaningful zero, and the group means should be positive. It is not suitable when the means have opposite signs or when zero is arbitrary, as with temperature measured in degrees Celsius.

### Step 3: consider the small-sample correction

The simple log ratio in @eq-lnrom-first-order can be biased, especially with small samples and large coefficients of variation. A second-order correction is [@Lajeunesse2015-gw]:

$$
L^{\ast}=\ln\left(\frac{m_1}{m_2}\right)
+\frac{1}{2}
\left(
\frac{s_1^2}{n_1m_1^2}
-\frac{s_2^2}{n_2m_2^2}
\right).
$${#eq-lnrom-second-order}

The added term becomes smaller as sample sizes increase. Unlike the pooled standard deviation in SMD, this term is a bias correction; it does not redefine lnRoM as a standardized mean difference.

Current versions of `metafor::escalc()` apply the lnRoM bias correction by default when `measure = "ROM"`. Use `correct = FALSE` to request the uncorrected value:

```{r}
# Bias-corrected lnRoM (the current default)
escalc(measure = "ROM",
       m1i = 12, sd1i = 3, n1i = 20,
       m2i = 10, sd2i = 2, n2i = 20)

# Uncorrected log ratio of means
escalc(measure = "ROM",
       m1i = 12, sd1i = 3, n1i = 20,
       m2i = 10, sd2i = 2, n2i = 20,
       correct = FALSE)
```

Because software defaults can change, record the package version, estimator options, and formulas used in every reproducible analysis.

We can now calculate both effect sizes for the plant-biomass example. Group 1 is the warming treatment and group 2 is the ambient control, following the direction chosen [above](#sec-direction-convention).

First calculate bias-corrected SMD (Hedges' $g$):

```{r}

#| echo: true
#| out-width: "70%"

escalc(m1i = 12, sd1i = 3, n1i = 20,
       m2i = 10, sd2i = 2, n2i = 20,
       measure = "SMD")

```

The expected SMD is approximately 0.77. In plain language, mean biomass in the warming treatment is about 0.77 pooled standard deviations higher than in the ambient control.

Values such as 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 are sometimes labelled “small,” “medium,” and “large.” These generic labels are not universal biological thresholds. Whether an SMD is biologically important depends on the response, organisms, intervention, study design, and consequences of the change. Whenever possible, interpret the estimate using domain knowledge and report its uncertainty.

Now calculate the uncorrected lnRoM from the same summary statistics:

```{r}
#| echo: true
#| eval: true
escalc(m1i = 12, sd1i = 3, n1i = 20,
       m2i = 10, sd2i = 2, n2i = 20,
       measure = "ROM",
       correct = FALSE)

```

The uncorrected lnRoM is $\ln(12/10)\approx0.182$. It can be converted to proportional or percentage change, with $y_i$ denoting lnRoM:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{proportional change} &= e^{y_i}-1,\\
\text{percentage change} &= 100(e^{y_i}-1).
\end{aligned}
$${#eq-lnrom-percent-change}

In `R`:

```{r}
#| echo: true
#| eval: true

(exp(log(12 / 10)) - 1) * 100

```

The result is a 20% increase in mean biomass under warming. This percentage interpretation is useful only when a proportional comparison is biologically meaningful and the response is measured on a ratio scale with a meaningful zero.

The two estimates answer the questions introduced at the start of the chapter:

- SMD: biomass is about 0.77 pooled standard deviations higher under warming.

- lnRoM: mean biomass is 20% higher under warming.

Neither statement is a better summary of the experiment in every context. The appropriate interpretation depends on whether the biological question concerns a difference relative to variation or a proportional difference.

The following properties help determine whether each interpretation is mathematically appropriate:

| Data property | SMD | lnRoM |
|---|---|---|
| Are group standard deviations part of the point estimate? | Yes | No, except in the small-sample bias correction |
| Must the response have a meaningful zero? | No | Yes |
| Must both group means be positive? | No | Yes |
| Can the estimate be back-transformed directly to a percentage? | No | Yes |

The remaining sections examine other considerations---including bounded responses, dimensionality, non-normality, overdispersion, heteroscedasticity, and missing standard deviations---without assuming that either effect size is generally more robust.

# Add a separate analysis of variability {#sec-lnrom-with-variability}

A treatment can change both the **mean** and the **variability** of a biological response [@Nakagawa2015-tq]. An lnRoM analysis describes the proportional change in the group means, but it does not tell us whether observations became more or less variable within the groups.

When variability is biologically relevant, we recommend adding a separate analysis using the **log variability ratio (lnVR)**:

$$
\mathrm{lnVR}=\ln\left(\frac{s_1}{s_2}\right),
$${#eq-lnvr}

where $s_1$ is the treatment-group standard deviation and $s_2$ is the control-group standard deviation. With the direction used throughout this chapter:

- $\mathrm{lnVR}>0$: the treatment group is more variable;

- $\mathrm{lnVR}=0$: the groups have equal standard deviations;

- $\mathrm{lnVR}<0$: the treatment group is less variable.

For the plant-biomass example:

$$
\mathrm{lnVR}=\ln\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)\approx0.405.
$$

Back-transforming gives $e^{0.405}\approx1.5$, so the standard deviation is approximately 50% higher under warming. The lnRoM and lnVR analyses together provide a more complete biological description: warming increased mean biomass by 20% and was also associated with greater variation among plants.

Calculate lnVR as a separate outcome rather than combining lnRoM and lnVR values in one univariate model:

```{r}
escalc(measure = "VR",
       sd1i = 3, n1i = 20,
       sd2i = 2, n2i = 20)
```

Interpret the two analyses separately. They address related but distinct questions: lnRoM concerns proportional differences in means, whereas lnVR concerns ratios of standard deviations.

:::callout-warning
### Use lnCVR carefully: its value depends directly on the means

The **log coefficient of variation ratio (lnCVR)** compares standard deviations after dividing each one by its group mean [@Nakagawa2015-tq]:

$$
\mathrm{lnCVR}
=\ln\left(\frac{s_1/m_1}{s_2/m_2}\right)
=\mathrm{lnVR}-\mathrm{lnRoM},
$${#eq-lncvr-relationship}

before any small-sample bias corrections. Because the means occur directly in this formula, a change in lnCVR can arise from a change in the standard deviations, a change in the means, or both.

lnCVR is intended to account for a common biological pattern: groups with larger means often also have larger variances. However, dividing by the mean makes an implicit scaling assumption. In simple terms, it works most naturally when the standard deviation increases roughly in proportion to the mean. Equivalently, variance then increases roughly with the square of the mean.

That relationship differs among data types:

- **Approximately normal data with constant variance:** the standard deviation may stay similar as the mean changes. The coefficient of variation will then change mechanically with the mean.

- **Poisson count data:** variance equals the mean, so standard deviation increases with the square root of the mean rather than directly with the mean. The coefficient of variation therefore decreases as the mean increases.

- **Negative-binomial count data:** variance can increase much faster than the mean. At large means, the standard deviation may be close to proportional to the mean, but the relationship depends on the dispersion parameter.

- **Proportions and other bounded responses:** variance is constrained by the mean and commonly shrinks near the boundaries. The mean–variance relationship can therefore be strong and nonlinear.

For these reasons, do not choose lnCVR automatically. First plot or model the relationship between group means and standard deviations across the dataset. Use lnCVR when *relative variability* is the intended estimand and its assumed mean–variance scaling is plausible. Otherwise, report lnVR alongside lnRoM, examine the mean–variance relationship directly, or consider an arm-based model of means and standard deviations.
:::

# When are SMD and lnRoM appropriate? {#sec-assumptions-and-properties}

## Values ≤ 0
A key consideration in choosing between SMD and lnRoM is whether your dataset has means ≤ 0. These may occur for a variety of reasons, including because of [before-after-control-impact](3.2-smd-challenges.qmd#sec-BACI) experimental designs or other data presentation formats. If means ≤ 0 are common in your dataset then you may have to use SMD simply in order to make use of all of your data.

:::callout-important
#### Data bounded between 0 and 1 {#sec-zero-one-bounded}

Means±SD bounded between 0 and 1 are challenged by their numeric properties: variance tends to retreat to zero when the mean approaches 0 or 1 [@Macartney2022-ik].

To resolve this for SMD and lnRoM effect sizes, you need to transform both the means and the standard deviations, using an arcsin-square root transformation on the mean and an equation derived using the delta method to standardize the standard deviation (see @Macartney2022-ik). 

We cover this transformation in our extraction challenges page [here](3.2-smd-challenges.qmd#sec-proportion-data-with-error) 
:::

## Dimensionality {#sec-dimensionality}

The effect sizes in this section are standardized, which allow us to compare results across studies even if their units were different. However, what if one study measured a response in meters but another study measured it in area ($meter^2$) and a third study measured the same response in volume ($meter^3$)? 

Imagine vegetation studies or morphological measurements of an organism or the size of a tumor. Depending on the effect size you choose, the results can be strongly affected by these added dimensions---which is an overlooked problem in meta-analysis.

For lnRoM, this is a very serious problem---the estimated effect at dimension 1 will double at dimension two!

For this reason, we suggest that if you are committed to using an lnRoM effect size, that you must record the number of dimensions of each measurement. You can use these to divide the resulting effect size point estimate by the number of dimensions to standardize dimensionality. E.g., if the measurement was across 2 dimensions ($m^2$) then divide by 2 and if across 3 dimensions ($m^3$), divide by 3. 

However, there may be many cases where the number of dimensions of the measurement may be unclear. In this case, you must use SMD. We demonstrate this in the hidden code block below.

## Overdispersion {#sec-overdispersion}

SMD and lnRoM assume that the underlying data generation process is normal. This assumption can be broken if the data is overdispersed---which is remarkably common in ecology and evolution, especially with abundance or count data. This can lead to biased point and sampling variance estimates.

There are as yet no effect sizes formulated for overdispersed data. As such, if there is overdispersed data in your dataset, you should:

- Exclude effect sizes that are overdispersed based on rules of thumb we'll give below as a sensitivity analysis in comparison to your full dataset

- Use the inverse of effective sample size as your meta-analytic weight, instead of sampling variance, as an additional sensitivity analysis.

As you'll see throughout this tutorial, sensitivity tests are the annoying reality of meta-analysis.

Testing for overdispersion should be part of your analytic [workflow](3.0-starting-extraction.qmd). The easiest way to test for overdispersion is to calculate the coefficient of variation ($CV$) for your control and treatment groups. $CV$ is the ratio of standard deviation to the mean ($sd/x$).

We consider a $CV>2$ to be indicative of overdispersion. However, for lnRoM, @Lajeunesse2015-gw suggested a small sample-size corrected threshold based on the standardized mean, derived from the Geary test [@Geary1930-yf, @Hedges1999-al]. Let $m$ be the group mean, $sd$ be the standard deviation and $n$ be your group sample size:

$$
\begin{aligned}
G' = \frac{m}{sd}*\frac{4*n^{3/2}}{1+4*n}
\end{aligned}
$${#eq-7}

There are several suggested thresholds for this value that can be used to exclude potentially overdispersed and problematic data. Lajuennesse suggested excluding values ≥3. 

If a lot of your data is overdispersed, one option is to compare the bias-corrected and uncorrected lnRoM estimators as a sensitivity analysis. In current versions of `metafor`, request the uncorrected estimator with `correct = FALSE`; it is not the default. We also recommend conducting sensitivity analyses that exclude data identified as potentially problematic using clearly specified criteria, and comparing sampling-variance weights with effective-sample-size weights when justified [@Nakagawa2023-tn].

<details>
<summary>Click to see a demonstration of overdispersion</summary>

Let's do this in `R` with a $CV>2$ threshold and the Geary-Lajuennesse threshold of 3, using a dataset provided by the `metafor` package:

```{r}
#| eval: true
#| echo: true

data("dat.gibson2002")
dat <- dat.gibson2002

# Drop NA values for the SMD type effect sizes
dat <- dat %>% filter(!is.na(m1i))

# Calculate coefficient of variation:
dat <- dat %>%
  mutate(cv1 = sd1i / m1i,
         cv2 = sd2i / m2i)

n_overdispersed <- dat %>% 
  filter(cv1 > 2 | cv2 > 2) %>%
  nrow()
n_overdispersed/nrow(dat) * 100

```

It looks like 46% of this dataset has a $CV>2$!

```{r}
#| eval: true
#| echo: true

# With the Geary-Lajuennesse threshold:
geary <- function(sd, m, n){
  (m/sd) * (4*n^(3/2))/(1 + 4*n)
}

dat <- dat %>%
  mutate(gl1 = geary(sd = sd1i, m = m1i, n = n1i),
         gl2 = geary(sd = sd2i, m = m2i, n = n2i))


n_overdispersed <- dat %>% 
  filter(gl1 > 3 | gl2 > 3) %>%
  nrow()
n_overdispersed/nrow(dat) * 100

```

It looks like \~54% the data in this meta-analysis fails the Geary-Lajuennesse test. So what do you do if that happens? We suggest that you filter out overdispersed values and rerun your models, both with the full dataset and with the subsets that are not overdispersed.

An alternative approach is to use the inverse of effective sample size as your meta-analytic model weight, instead of sampling variance. This may be an essential method when a significant amount of your data is overdispersed. This is a method that produces reasonable meta-analytic estimates, despite ignoring standard deviation [@Nakagawa2023-tn].

Effective sample size is defined as: 
$$
\begin{aligned}
N_{0} = \frac{4n_1n_2}{n_1 + n_2}\\
\end{aligned}
$${#eq-8}

Let's go through a full example, with lnRoM as our effect size.

First we'll calculate lnRoM point estimates and sampling variance:

```{r}
#| eval: true
#| echo: true

dat <- escalc(measure = "ROM", 
              m1i = m1i, m2i = m2i, 
              sd1i = sd1i, sd2i = sd2i,
              n1i = n1i, n2i = n2i,
              data = dat, append = TRUE) |> 
  setDT()
head(dat)

```

Now let's run an intercept only model with `vi` as our meta-analytic weight (taken by the argument `V`). Note that there is only one effect size per article, which is reflected in our random effect formula (which lacks an effect size ID nested within the article id `author`):

```{r}
#| eval: true
#| echo: true

m1 <- rma.mv(yi, 
             V = vi,
             random = ~ 1 | author,
             data = dat)
```

Now, let's run a model excluding the overdispersed data:

```{r}
# Now let's run a sensitivity analysis by excluding overdispersed data
sub.dat <- dat %>% 
  filter(cv1 <= 2 & cv2 <= 2)
m2 <- rma.mv(yi, 
             V = vi,
             random = ~ 1 | author,
             data = sub.dat)
```

Finally, let's run a model using the inverse of effective sample size as our weight.

```{r}
dat <- dat %>%
  mutate(effective_N = (4*n1i*n2i)/(n1i+n2i))

m3 <- rma.mv(yi, 
             V = 1/effective_N,
             random = ~ 1 | author,
             data = dat)
```

Let's compare these:

```{r}

#| echo: false

compare_estimates <- rbind(m1 %>% 
                             tidy() %>% 
                             mutate(model = "all data",
                                    ci.lb = m1$ci.lb,
                                    ci.ub = m1$ci.ub),
                           m2 %>%
                             tidy() %>%
                             mutate(model = "CV <= 2",
                                    ci.lb = m3$ci.lb,
                                    ci.ub = m3$ci.ub),
                           m3 %>%
                             tidy() %>%
                             mutate(model = "1/effective_N",
                                    ci.lb = m3$ci.lb,
                                    ci.ub = m3$ci.ub))
compare_estimates$model <- factor(compare_estimates$model,
                                  levels = c("all data", "CV <= 2", "1/effective_N"))

ggplot(data = compare_estimates,
       aes(x = model, y = estimate, ymin = ci.lb, ymax = ci.ub))+
  ylab("Estimate ± 95% CIs")+
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0)+
  geom_pointrange()+
  theme_bw()

```

It looks like the sensitivity analyses (CV ≤ 2 and 1/effective_N) both produced similar results which were more conservative than when the entire dataset was analyzed.

</details>

## Heteroscedasticity {#sec-heteroscedasticity}

Heteroscedasticity (i.e., differences in variance between the experimental groups) can bias effect sizes considerably. SMD has a special formulation that accounts for heteroscedasticity [@Bonett2009-sf]. This is available as the measure 'SMDH' in `metafor::escalc`.

For the uncorrected lnRoM, the point estimate depends only on the two means, so unequal group variances do not change that point estimate. However, the group variances do affect the sampling variance and the small-sample bias correction. Heteroscedasticity therefore still matters for uncertainty and for the choice of estimator.


# Conclusion---which effect size should you use?

Return first to the biological question. Use SMD when the difference between groups should be expressed relative to variation among observations. Use lnRoM when the proportional difference between positive means is the quantity of interest and the response has a meaningful zero. The flow chart below summarizes additional considerations.

![SMD vs lnRoM](figures/flowcharts_diagrams/SMD vs lnrom.jpg)

When both estimands are biologically meaningful, analyzing SMD and lnRoM separately can reveal whether the conclusion depends on how the effect was expressed. This is not merely a statistical robustness check: the two analyses answer different questions and should be interpreted accordingly.

:::callout-note
@Yang2024-fw showed that including both SMD and lnRoM in a bivariate model can improve model estimates. See their tutorial [here](https://yefeng0920.github.io/BRMA_tutorial_git/).
:::

For lnRoM, state whether the small-sample bias correction was applied. Current versions of `metafor::escalc()` apply it by default. The uncorrected estimator can be requested with `correct = FALSE`; if it is used because of concerns about distributional assumptions, justify that choice and compare estimators in a sensitivity analysis.

```{r}
#| eval: true
#| echo: true
#| out-width: "70%"

eff_size(effect_type = "lnRoM",
         x1 = 15, x2 = 10,
         sd1 = 1.5, sd2 = 1.5,
         n1 = 15, n2 = 15,
         default_formulas = FALSE)

```

Finally, if data are identified as potentially problematic using the criteria described [above](#sec-overdispersion), compare the full analysis with clearly justified sensitivity analyses, such as excluding flagged observations or using inverse effective sample size as the model weight [@Nakagawa2023-tn].

:::callout-important
#### Missing error bars

Note that some extraction challenges may also influence your choice of effect size. For example, it is possible to calculate lnRoM for data that did not report error (e.g., standard deviation, standard error, etc) by imputing error with cross-study coefficient of variation [@Nakagawa2023-tn]. This is a good reason to use lnRoM but has to be weighed against the other numeric assumptions described above. We discuss this further [here](3.2-smd-challenges.qmd#sec-callout-missing-error-bars).

# Alternative estimators {#sec-alternative-estimators}

## Paired designs {#sec-paired-designs}

There are alternative estimators for SMD and lnRoM can account for *paired* designs, that is for correlated variance between groups 1 and 2. 

In some cases, effect sizes derived from paired designs can be compared directly to those from unpaired designs. However, this is not always clear. There are a myriad of estimators for SMD-style effect sizes that account for pre-post treatment designs (see our `escalc` [cheatsheet](6-escalc-cheatsheet.qmd)). These options may preferable if your meta-analysis focuses only on these types of experiments.

However, these estimators are **not** comparable to normal, unpaired SMD estimators. Our `eff_size` function provides a paired option for SMD that is comparable to unpaired SMD estimates, which we demonstrate below. `escalc` (as well as `eff_size`) provides an paired lnRoM estimator that is comparable to the standard unpaired estimator, which we also demonstrate below.

In paired design estimators, the variance estimation formulas accept an additional argument $\rho$---the correlation between plots or individuals, which is pronounced "rho" and symbolized as `r` in most `R` code. Since $\rho$ is usually unknown and unreported, it's general practice to set it to 0.5 or 0.8 and to run sensitivity analyses to ensure that that choice isn't unduly influencing your model estimates.

::: callout-note
######### $\rho$ {#sec-specifying-rho}

The correlation between groups, $\rho$ (`ri` in `escalc` and `r` in `eff_size`) is rarely reported and so needs to be assigned based, basically, on guesswork. We'd suggest that if the pairing is within an individual or plot (e.g., before-after a treatment), then a $\rho$ of 0.8 seems reasonable. However, if the pairing is based on spatially paired but independent plots (e.g., adjacent to each other across a fenceline) then a rho of 0.5 may be more reasonable.

Be sure to always conduct sensitivity analyses on these choices, to ensure that they aren't unduly changing your model estimates.
:::

If groups 1 and 2 are paired (e.g., in a before-after design), you can calculate a paired lnRoM with the following code. Note that you only supply a single value for sample size (`ni`) instead of `n1i` and `n2i` because a paired design must by definition have equal sample sizes in each treatment. Paired lnRoMs will have the same point estimate (yi) as their unpaired flavor but will have lower variance and thus higher weight in your meta-analytic models.

```{r}
#| echo: true
#| eval: false

# First, unpaired:
unpaired <- escalc(m1i = 7, sd1i = 1.2, 
                    m2i = 12, sd2i = 0.9,
                    n1i = 15, n2i = 15,
                    measure = "ROM",
                    var.names = c("yi_unpaired", "vi_unpaired"))


# Now, the paired version:
paired <- escalc(m1i = 7, sd1i = 1.2, 
                  m2i = 12, sd2i = 0.9,
                  ni = 15, ri = 0.8,
                  measure = "ROMC",
                  var.names = c("yi_paired", "vi_paired"))

cbind(unpaired, paired)

```

As you can see, the point estimate remained the same but the variance was reduced in the paired formulation, leading to a stronger influence in the meta-analytic models.

To calculate a paired SMD estimate, you need to use our function. Here we'll calculate paired and unpaired estimates:

```{r}
#| echo: true
#| eval: false

unpaired <- eff_size(x1 = 7, sd1 = 1.2, 
                      x2 = 12, sd2 = 0.9, 
                      n1 = 15, n2 = 15,
                      paired = FALSE,
                      verbose = FALSE,
                      effect_type = "SMD")
setnames(unpaired, c("yi", "vi"), c("yi_unpaired", "vi_unpaired"))

paired <- eff_size(x1 = 7, sd1 = 1.2, 
                    x2 = 12, sd2 = 0.9, 
                    n = 15, r = 0.5,
                    paired = TRUE,
                    verbose = FALSE,
                    effect_type = "SMD")
setnames(paired, c("yi", "vi"), c("yi_paired", "vi_paired"))

cbind(unpaired, paired)

```

As you can see, the paired version reduced the sampling variance but not the point estimate. The formula for paired SMD point estimates and sampling variance we used in `eff_size` are here:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&J = 1 - \frac{3}{4 * (2n - 2) - 1} \\
&sd_{pooled} = \sqrt{\frac{sd_1^2(n-1) + sd_2^2(n-1)}{2n - 2}} \\
&d = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{sd_{pooled}} \\
&g = J * d \\
&Var(g) = \frac{2 * (1-r)}{n} + \frac{d^2}{2 * (n-1)} \\
\end{aligned}
$${#eq-9}
